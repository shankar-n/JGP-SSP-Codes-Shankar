\begin{abstract}
\setstretch{1.15}
\noindent
A machine that makes many different parts needs many different tools, but it can
hold only a few of them at a time. Running a set of jobs therefore means stopping to
change tools, and each stop costs production time. The Job Sequencing and Tool
Switching Problem (SSP) asks for the order of jobs, and the tool loading at each
step, that makes the total number of tool changes as small as possible. Choosing the
loading for a fixed order is easy. Choosing the order is NP-hard, and it is where all
of the difficulty sits.

In practice the SSP is not attacked directly. Jobs are first grouped into as few
magazine-fitting batches as possible, and the batches are then sequenced. This
two-phase method is fast and is used in industrial practice, and \citet{CramaKlundert1999}
showed it to be a $b$-approximation, where $b$ is the magazine capacity. This report
sharpens that. The method admits two readings that the
literature has not separated --- the cost of a walk through one fixed magazine per
group, and the cost obtained once its final step re-optimises the
loading --- and the two differ on the instances most often used as examples. Within
that setting, the optimum is shown to equal the cheapest walk over \emph{all}
groupings, so the two-phase gap --- measured on the best value the construction can
reach, not on a particular implementation of it --- is exactly the price of insisting on
the fewest groups; classes of instances with zero gap are identified for every capacity; upper
bounds on the gap are proved for every instance; and the extremal behaviour is worked
out at three groups. In the finite censuses run here, positive gaps are rare, every one
observed in them equals one, and none occurs at a magazine capacity of two; larger gaps
are reached only by disconnected constructions. That regime agrees with the one
published industrial study. A parameterised cost model places the SSP and the grouping
problem at the two ends of a single spectrum, with the transition governed by the gap
itself.

On the exact side the obstacle is a single quantity. The strongest compact formulation
in the literature has a linear relaxation equal to the number of tools minus the
magazine capacity, which is an elementary counting bound. Two methods are built to get
past it. A branch-and-Benders-cut algorithm places the ordering in a master problem and
settles the loading exactly with a polynomial oracle, so it never forms a weak
relaxation. Position-indexed formulations replace the configuration walk by a fixed row
set of polynomial size, and one of them, the position--transition formulation, has a
linear relaxation that can provably exceed the counting bound.

Both were implemented and benchmarked on $1{,}421$ instances under one uniform
protocol, against three compact models from the literature reimplemented here. That
baseline is not the literature at its strongest --- Formulation~4 of
\citet{Catanzaro2015} is used where its authors recommend Formulation~5 --- and it
closes more instances than anything built here regardless, so what the campaign yields
is a diagnosis rather than a faster solver. For any method whose bound is that counting
bound, whether it happens to equal the optimum decides the outcome: $94\%$ of the
instances where it is tight are closed, against $40\%$ of the rest, and where the solver
fails it has usually already found the optimum and cannot certify it. The split belongs
to the methods and not to the instances --- an arc-based compact model, whose relaxation
is a different quantity, shows no such collapse and closes every one of the instances
where the counting bound is loosest. Of four standard strengthenings only the one
supplying a better incumbent helps, and the branch-and-price prototypes, run to read
their relaxation value rather than to compete, report the same bound from the other
direction.

The cause is structural rather than incidental: the objective counts tool
re-insertions, produced by the global interleaving of the schedule, while every
inequality either method carries is supported on a single arc or a single position. A
family that is not local is derived, proved valid, and shown --- by computing what each
family could deliver at best --- to have roughly three and a half times the remaining headroom of
the family the solvers carry today.
\end{abstract}

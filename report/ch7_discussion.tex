\section{Discussion}\label{sec:disc}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Certificate-limited in the tested Benders implementation}\label{ssec:disc-bound}

A method can fail to solve an instance for two reasons. It can fail to find a good
solution, or it can find one and fail to prove that no better one exists. The
distinction matters because the two failures call for different remedies. The first
is answered by better search: heuristics, warm starts, branching rules. The second is
answered only by a better bound.

For the tested Benders regimes, four independent measurements separate the two cases,
and all four point the same way.

\emph{The incumbent at the time limit.} On the runs that hit the limit and whose
optimum is known from elsewhere, the Benders solver's incumbent already equals that
optimum $65\%$ of the time across its nine configurations, and $58\%$ of the time for
the plain one. In those runs the solver has found the answer and spends the remaining
hour failing to certify it.

\emph{The ablation.} Four strengthenings were implemented. The one that improves the
incumbent, a hybrid genetic search run at the root, gains thirty-one instances. The
three that aim at the dual bound --- fractional cuts, Pareto-optimal lifting, a
conflict-graph row --- are neutral or harmful; fractional separation costs sixty-nine
solved instances and Pareto lifting costs thirty-seven. A solver whose difficulty was finding good solutions would not behave
this way.

\emph{The split by bound tightness.} The same solver closes $94\%$ of the instances on
which the elementary bound $q$ happens to equal the optimum, and $40\%$ of those on
which it does not. The classification is by a single structural property rather than by
size or density, and it is the property Section~\ref{sec:gap} identifies as governing
the problem. The split is not evidence that the loose instances are intrinsically hard:
the arc-based \texttt{CATZ-F4}, whose relaxation is not $q$, closes a \emph{larger}
share of the loose instances than of the tight ones and closes all $45$ of the loosest
(Table~\ref{tab:tightness}). Nor does every method whose relaxation equals $q$ collapse
--- SSPMF loses only $13$ points across the same split. What the $54$-point loss
measures is a master that carries $q$ and, at the root, almost nothing else. That makes
it evidence about this way of building a solver rather than about the SSP.

\emph{The other method.} The branch-and-price prototype reports its root relaxation on
every run, and on all $2{,}108$ runs of the position--configuration formulation that
value is exactly $q$. A different algorithm, a different formulation and a different
solver arrive at the same number.

The natural objection is that this is a statement about one implementation. It is not,
and Proposition~\ref{prop:pcf} is why: the position--configuration relaxation, arrived
at from a completely different direction, equals $q$ exactly. So does the multicommodity
relaxation of \citet{daSilva2024}, proved by its own authors. Three independent routes
reach the same value. A configuration-based exact method for the SSP is therefore
limited by the strength of its bound and not by its implementation.

The fractional-cut result is the strongest form of this conclusion. Separating at
fractional points is the standard remedy when a Benders scheme tails off, and it does
what it is supposed to do locally: almost sixty-seven million cuts reduce the node count by a
factor of twenty. It nonetheless raises the bound at termination on no instance at all.
The remedy is being applied correctly, to the wrong obstacle.

\subsection{Where a stronger bound could come from}\label{ssec:disc-locality}

Section~\ref{ssec:disc-bound} establishes that the methods of this report are limited
by their bound. That is a diagnosis of the symptom. This subsection asks what causes
it, and measures what a different family of inequalities could be worth. The evidence
points to a structural locality mismatch; that is an explanation consistent with the
ablation, not a theorem of causation.

\paragraph{The cost is not a local quantity.}
Write $\beta_t$ for the number of maximal blocks of consecutive positions during which
tool $t$ sits in the magazine. In the empty-start convention every insertion of $t$
begins one such block. Theory here uses the free-initial convention, so the exact
identity is
\[
  \Zst
  = \sum_{t\in\U}\beta_t-\min(b,\lvert\U\rvert).
\]
When $\lvert\U\rvert\ge b$, as on the nontrivial benchmark instances, this becomes
\[
  \Zst=q+\sum_{t\in\U}(\beta_t-1).
\]
Thus one block per used tool recovers the coverage bound exactly, and everything above
$q$ is a
\emph{re-insertion}: a tool evicted and brought back. A re-insertion happens when the
jobs needing a tool are pushed apart by other jobs whose requirements cannot sit
alongside it. That is a property of the global interleaving of the schedule. It is not
a property of which pairs of jobs happen to be adjacent.

\paragraph{The original master information is locally indexed.}
Against that, consider what the two methods actually give their relaxations.

The Benders master carries one row $\theta\ge\sum_{ij}w_{ij}x_{ij}$, which is indexed
by arcs; the triplet rows $\theta\ge w_{ijk}(x_{ij}+x_{jk}-1)$, which are the weak
linearisation of a product and are worth nothing at a fractional point where both arcs
sit at one half; and the constant coverage row. Every optimality cut it generates is
also arc-supported, with coefficient $\sum_t\lambda_{ijt}$ on $x_{ij}$. So the master
learns only adjacency-indexed facts about a cost that is not adjacency-indexed.

The position--configuration relaxation prices one configuration per position and
couples positions only through per-tool presence rows, so its linear relaxation mixes
configurations fractionally at each position and pays no transition. That is why
Proposition~\ref{prop:pcf} holds, and it is why the measured root value is $q$ on every
one of $2{,}108$ runs.

PTF is the one construction here that ever exceeds $q$, and the reason is the same one
stated positively: a column carries a transition, so the relaxation cannot avoid paying
for it. Why it exceeds $q$ so rarely is not established. The candidate explanation is
that its chaining between consecutive pairs is enforced tool by tool rather than
configuration by configuration, which would restore the fractional mixing one level up;
that is a conjecture about the formulation, and testing it means building the stronger
chaining and measuring.

\begin{observation}[The coverage row is carrying the master alone]\label{obs:rootq}
On $9{,}067$ of the $10{,}392$ canonical Benders runs for which a root relaxation value was
recorded --- $87.2\%$ --- that value equals $\lvert\U\rvert$ exactly. The pairwise row
and the triplet rows therefore deliver no improvement beyond coverage on almost nine
runs in ten. Where
the pairwise structure does bite it bites hard, by $7.71$ on average, on the $1{,}325$
runs where it moves the root at all. Which instances those are is not characterised
here.
\end{observation}

\paragraph{A family that is not local.}
Let $W=[p,r]$ be a contiguous block of positions with $p\ge2$. Write $w_{t,k}$ for the
insertion of tool $t$ at position $k$ and $a_{t,k}$ for its presence, as in the
position-indexed formulation of Section~\ref{ssec:pos}.

\begin{proposition}[Integral window inequality]\label{prop:window}
For every contiguous block of positions $W=[p,r]$ and every integer-feasible
trajectory of PCF,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:window}
  \sum_{t\in\U}\ \sum_{k=p}^{r} w_{t,k}
  \;\;\ge\;\;
  \bigl\lvert\{t: a_{t,k}=1 \text{ for some } k\in W\}\bigr\rvert
  \;-\; \sum_{t\in\U} a_{t,p-1}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $S$ be the set of tools present at some position of $W$ and $A$ the set present at
position $p-1$. Fix $t\in S\setminus A$. Then $a_{t,p-1}=0$ and $a_{t,k}=1$ for some
$k\in W$, so there is a smallest such $k$, and at it $a_{t,k}-a_{t,k-1}=1$. Constraint
(W) gives $w_{t,k}\ge1$. Distinct tools contribute to distinct terms, so the left-hand
side is at least $\lvert S\setminus A\rvert\ge\lvert S\rvert-\lvert A\rvert$, which is
the right-hand side.
\end{proof}

This is valid integral accounting, but it does not by itself strengthen the
fractional master. The implemented robust extension introduces
$v_{t,W}\ge a_{t,k}$ for $k\in W$ and replaces the cardinality by
$\sum_t v_{t,W}$. Its best choice is
$v_{t,W}=\max_{k\in W}a_{t,k}$. The resulting fractional inequality is redundant:
for every tool and every $h\in W$, the existing rows (W) and $w\ge0$ imply
\[
 \sum_{k=p}^{r}w_{t,k}\ \ge\
 \sum_{k=p}^{h}(a_{t,k}-a_{t,k-1})
 =a_{t,h}-a_{t,p-1}.
\]
Taking the maximum over $h$ and summing over tools gives exactly the implemented
window row. It therefore cannot raise the PCF relaxation. In particular, it is not a
fractional generalisation of the counting rows (T), whose right-hand side requires
every used tool to reach one somewhere rather than merely its fractional maximum.

The implementation was additionally checked against the loading rule at $1{,}200{,}816$
integer points --- every window of every sequence of a bank of random instances --- with
no violation. That is a test of the code, not of the proposition.

\paragraph{What it would be worth.}
A candidate construction is worth implementing only if its integral-sequence value is
materially better than the bound already available. That can be screened before writing
solver code. On an instance small enough to enumerate, the value of the fixed
Tang--Denardo pairwise row is obtained by solving its Hamiltonian path problem and then
taking the better of it and coverage. For the window family, the strongest disjoint
window decomposition of each sequence is found by an $O(n^2)$ dynamic program and then
minimised over sequences. These are ceilings for those two specific constructions over
integral sequences. In particular, the pairwise value is \emph{not} a ceiling for every
arc-supported Benders cut.
Table~\ref{tab:ceilings} reports both on the $62$ loose cases within the fixed
$81$-instance Laporte3 diagnostic sample listed in
\texttt{verification/bound\_probe\_instances.txt}. The sample makes the exact
enumeration reproducible; it is a probe of the two families, not a census or an
estimate over all $340$ Laporte3 instances.

Two readings follow. The fixed pairwise surrogate is weak on this sample: on three
quarters of the loose cases it adds nothing to coverage. The window construction has a
larger integral-sequence value, roughly two fifths of the remaining gap at the median.
Neither statement predicts the value of a fractional master relaxation; that is tested
directly next for the window rows.

\begin{observation}[The redundant window extension changes no tested root]\label{obs:windowroot}
The family of Proposition~\ref{prop:window} was added to the PCF$'$ master --- $15$
windows of length two to four, which at $n=8$ is $660$ to $1{,}090$ rows and $225$ to
$375$ auxiliary variables depending on $\lvert T\rvert$ ---
and the root relaxation recomputed on $38$ of the $62$ loose Laporte3 instances, of
which $37$ returned a value and one, \texttt{L18-4}, did not. The root
value equals $q$ on all $37$ with the rows and without them; the family raises the bound
on none, including instances whose gap is ten or eleven. Every added row is slack at the
returned LP optimum, by between $0.5$ and $3.4$. This experiment is now a regression
check of the redundancy argument above, not evidence that the family might strengthen
some other PCF root.
\end{observation}

\paragraph{What a real strengthening must add.}
The implemented max-presence row belongs nowhere: it is already implied. A useful
position-indexed strengthening must instead encode whether a tool is required
\emph{somewhere} in a window and tie that union to job coverage strongly enough to
survive fractional mixing, or enforce configuration-level rather than tool-level
trajectory consistency. Either route introduces non-robust rows or richer columns and
therefore changes the pricing problem. Expressing the same idea in the Benders
arc master is harder still because contiguity of a job set is a product of arc
indicators. The failed experiment is useful precisely because the redundancy proof
now rules out the cheap robust version.

\subsection{Where the theory and the measurements meet}\label{ssec:disc-agree}

Three agreements between Section~\ref{sec:gap} and Section~\ref{sec:comp} are worth
drawing out, because each was arrived at independently.

\paragraph{The bound governs both sides.}
Section~\ref{sec:gap} shows that $q$ equals the optimum on a large part of the
instance space, and that where it does the two-phase heuristic tends to be exact.
Section~\ref{sec:comp} shows that where it does, the methods built here close at the
root, and where it does not they branch without closing. The same quantity sits on both
sides. The association is what is measured; the wording should not promote it to a
mechanism, and Section~\ref{ssec:results-tight} shows why: \texttt{CATZ-F4}, whose
relaxation is not $q$, closes a larger share of the loose instances than of the tight
ones, so a tight $q$ does not make an exact method fast in general --- it makes the
methods whose bound is $q$ fast. Restricted that way the coincidence still bites, and
unpleasantly: the instances on which one would most want a configuration-based exact
method are exactly the instances on which it is weakest.

\paragraph{The gap opens when the magazine has slack.}
Observation~\ref{obs:census} gives an explicit positive-gap witness at $b=3$ and finds
none in the fixed $1{,}260$-instance sample at three to five jobs. Independently,
\citet{Burger2015} report from an industrial study that the decomposition does well
when the largest job nearly fills the magazine, and that its solution quality
decreases as the gap between the magazine size and the largest job size grows. One is
an exact witness plus a fixed small-instance census and the other is a case study on a
printing press. They describe the same regime.

\paragraph{The walk model overstates the weakness of the standard method.}
This is the least expected of the three. The literature's mental model of the
two-phase heuristic is a walk through one magazine per group, and under that model the
canonical example family has a positive gap that grows without bound. Under the method
as it is actually specified, with its final loading step, that same family has no gap
at all (Observation~\ref{obs:rings}). The standard method is better than the standard
picture of it. Problem~\ref{prob:ktns-closes} asks how much better, and that question
did not exist before the two readings were separated.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{ssec:limits}

Seven, ordered by how much they threaten the conclusions above.

\paragraph{The census of heuristic gaps is on small instances.}
Observation~\ref{obs:census} reports one fixed random census: $1{,}260$ instances at
three to five jobs, with no positive gap. Exploratory searches at larger sizes supplied
checked witnesses, including one at $b=3$, but their exact random manifests were not
retained; the report therefore makes no numerical frequency claim from them and no
absence claim at $b=2$. Nothing in the fixed census shows the frequency at industrial
sizes, and the qualitative agreement with \citet{Burger2015} comes from a different
method rather than a wider range of sizes. Theorem~\ref{thm:connected-gap} now settles
existence and unboundedness on connected instances, but says nothing about how common
such gaps are. The structural results of
Sections~\ref{ssec:exact} and \ref{ssec:ratio} do not depend on it; those are proved for
every instance and every capacity.

\paragraph{An integral-sequence ceiling is not an LP bound.}
Table~\ref{tab:ceilings} and Figure~\ref{fig:ceilings} give two values found by
enumerating every sequence of the $62$ loose cases in a fixed $81$-instance sample at
eight jobs. The comparison is reproducible and useful as a screen, but the pairwise row
is not representative of all arc-supported cuts and the sample is not a family-wide
estimate.
The window extension was added to PCF$'$ and raises none of the $37$ roots with paired
values (Observation~\ref{obs:windowroot}). The theorem audit gives the stronger
explanation: its fractional row is implied by existing transition rows. The
integral-sequence ceiling and the implemented LP extension are different objects; a
future strengthening must first prove that its fractional encoding is not redundant.

\paragraph{The raw Catanzaro directory was not the published benchmark.}
The campaign ran $171$ files from that directory, but the cited benchmark defines
$160$. Ten extra files duplicate canonical cases byte for byte and one is an ad-hoc
smoke test. Keeping them would give those ten matrices double weight and attach an
unsupported provenance to the eleventh, so every aggregate in this report filters the
eleven identities explicitly. The $132$ excluded result rows remain in the raw archive,
and the analysis checks both the exclusion count and the canonical total before
computing a figure. No solver result is invented or discarded on its outcome.

\paragraph{Primary outcomes and memory sensitivity are separate ledgers.}
The completed 16-GiB primary ledger contains $376$ scheduler-confirmed memory-limit
outcomes: $352$ for LSS and $24$ for Benders. Scheduler evidence establishes the cause,
including the $15$ recovery tasks that exhausted the same 16-GiB envelope. These
outcomes remain failures in the primary denominator. A separate 32-GiB LSS sensitivity reran all
positions; at the $352$ primary OOM identities it produced $245$ time-limit-feasible
rows and $107$ further memory failures, but no additional optimum. Those rows are
\emph{not} merged into the primary comparison.

The $123$ primary rows selected for exact-envelope recovery comprised $58$ absent
identities, $48$ filesystem-credential expiries, and $17$ unexplained worker exits.
Their manifest-driven 16-GiB rerun is now validated and merged: $17$ certified optima,
$91$ time-limit-feasible outcomes, and $15$ scheduler-confirmed OOM outcomes. Thus
Table~\ref{tab:solves} is the balanced final primary comparison. The separate 32-GiB
sensitivity is not used in it.

\paragraph{The comparison is one hour, one machine, one collection, and F4 rather than F5.}
Every solve count and time in Section~\ref{sec:comp} comes from a single one-hour limit
on one homogeneous node type over the standard collections;
Figure~\ref{fig:cactus} shows how the counts would move under a longer limit and that
they would not move equally for all methods. \citet{Catanzaro2015} recommend
Formulation~5, which was not implemented, so the compact baseline here is weaker than
the best available. Both points run against this report's own conclusion rather than for
it, and neither touches the bound results, which concern relaxation values rather than
solver speed.

\paragraph{The branch-and-price prototypes run in Python; the baselines run in C.}
A node of a Python pricer costs orders of magnitude more than a node inside CPLEX, and
the counts in Table~\ref{tab:bnp} are additionally outcome-dependent through the
early-stop rule. Nothing above nine jobs was closed, and that carries no information
about the formulations. None of the conclusions drawn from these runs uses a solve
count: they rest on the root relaxation value, recorded before any search and so
affected by neither problem.

\paragraph{The learned cut-selection prototype was not run on the SSP.}
It was trained and measured on knapsack cover cuts, with one seed per size and no
repetition. It shows that the agent learns, and says nothing about this problem. It is
therefore reported in Appendix~\ref{app:rl} rather than among the methods. That bears
on Appendix~\ref{app:rl} and nothing else, and
Section~\ref{ssec:disc-locality} explains why running it here would not have helped:
cut selection chooses among cuts a method can already generate. In this campaign the
available selections did not improve the terminal bound; the fixed-pairwise probe is
not used as a general ceiling argument.

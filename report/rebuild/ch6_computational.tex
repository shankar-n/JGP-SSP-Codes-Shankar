%==============================================================================
\section{Computational study}\label{sec:comp}
%==============================================================================

Computation here is how the theory is tested. The quantity the
structural results single out, the coverage bound $q$, is also the root relaxation of
every configuration-based method, so a benchmark that records optima and bounds
measures the theory's central variable directly. The campaign also settles a question
that predates the theory and motivated the Benders solver in the first place: the SSP
has a polynomially solvable loading subproblem, which is the textbook precondition for
Benders decomposition, and whether a decomposition can exploit it against modern
compact models had not been tested.

\subsection{Experimental design}\label{ssec:bench}

\paragraph{Instances.}
The benchmark uses the standard SSP instance families, taken from the collection
assembled by \citet{Otiai2024}: the sets of \citet{Catanzaro2015}, of
\citet{Crama1994}, and four of the sets of \citet{Laporte2004}. Every instance in
every family is run; none is skipped, and no instance is selected on the basis of an
outcome. Table~\ref{tab:families} gives the sizes.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
Every run is given a single uniform time limit of one hour and $16$ gigabytes of
memory. There is no early stopping and no per-configuration retirement rule, so the
number of instances solved is reported against the full family size rather than
against an outcome-dependent count of attempts. All other solver parameters are left
at their defaults, so that the comparison measures the formulations rather than
parameter tuning.

\paragraph{Hardware and software.}
All runs were executed on the LIMOS SLURM cluster, confined to a homogeneous pool of
identical nodes (AMD EPYC~7452, $2\times32$ cores, $512$\,GB of memory), so that
wall-clock times are comparable across runs. The mixed-integer solver is CPLEX~22.1.1
throughout, pinned to four dedicated threads per run. The branch-and-price prototypes
run single-threaded under PySCIPOpt.

\paragraph{Configurations.}
Eleven solver configurations were run. Three are the reimplemented compact baselines:
F4 of \citet{Catanzaro2015}, LSS of \citet{Laporte2004}, and SSPMF of
\citet{daSilva2024}. The remaining eight are the Benders solver, in an ablation over
its cut families and its strengthenings. The naming is as follows: \texttt{BBC-LP}
uses the dual optimality cuts, \texttt{BBC-K} the combinatorial cuts, \texttt{+T} adds
the triplet bounds, \texttt{+F} enables fractional separation, \texttt{+C} the
conflict-graph row, \texttt{+H} the hybrid-genetic incumbent, \texttt{+P} the
Pareto-optimal cut lifting, and \texttt{+ACC} all three strengthenings together.

\paragraph{The reported quantity.}
The common metric is the empty-start cost of the sequence a method returns, evaluated
by the loading oracle. This is well defined for every method regardless of its native
objective, and it is what makes the cross-method comparison of
Section~\ref{ssec:validation} meaningful.

\subsection{Validation}\label{ssec:validation}

Correctness was established before any result was recorded, and again from the results
themselves.

\emph{Before the campaign.} Every exact solver was checked against brute-force optima
on a bank of small instances, in every combination of its option flags. The loading
oracle itself was verified against an exact dynamic program over magazine states; the
verification described in Appendix~\ref{app:verify} repeats this on $83{,}670$
instance-and-sequence pairs and finds no disagreement.

\emph{During the campaign.} The harness converts every native objective to the common
empty-start cost before any comparison. Across every instance solved to optimality by
more than one method --- $974$ instances --- no two methods ever disagree on that cost.

\emph{A defect found and corrected.} The first campaign gave the Benders solver poor
results. The cause was that its master problem lacked the coverage row
$\theta\ge\lvert\U\rvert$ of Section~\ref{ssec:bbc}, which every other method carries
implicitly through its formulation. The row was added and the whole campaign re-run.
All numbers below come from the corrected solver. The episode is reported because it
is the reason the earlier interim results were superseded, and because it illustrates
the general point of Section~\ref{sec:disc}: on this problem a missing bound is
expensive in a way that a missing search improvement is not.

\subsection{How far each method gets}\label{ssec:results-solved}

\paragraph{The campaign is still running.}
The runs are distributed over the cluster as independent jobs, and the campaign had
not finished when this section was prepared. What follows is therefore a snapshot: it
covers the runs completed so far, and not every configuration yet has a result on
every instance.

solved and the number attempted for each configuration, and every comparison between
two configurations in this section is made on the instances that both attempted.
Absolute solved counts across configurations with different denominators are not
comparable, and are not compared. The conclusions of Section~\ref{sec:disc} rest on
those paired comparisons and on the split of Section~\ref{ssec:results-tight}, both of

Three things can be read from Table~\ref{tab:solves}.

The multicommodity model of \citet{daSilva2024} is the strongest single method, as its
authors report. That their claim replicates on an independent implementation and a
different instance collection is worth recording in its own right.

The Laporte3 family is solved completely by every method, and Laporte4 splits the
methods sharply: the compact models solve all of it, and the Benders solver solves
about three-fifths. The Catanzaro family is the hardest for every method.

The Benders variants behave almost identically to one another except for the
fractional-separation ones, which are worse. Section~\ref{ssec:results-bbc} takes that
apart.

\subsection{The Benders solver and its strengthenings}\label{ssec:results-bbc}

\paragraph{Fractional cuts.}
Section~\ref{ssec:accel} recorded that fractional separation had been inert in the
earlier campaign because of a defect in the cut-injection call, so the idea could not
be tested. The defect is corrected, and the corrected version was run.

The cuts are now generated in very large numbers. Across the $1{,}247$ instances that
both \texttt{BBC-LP} and \texttt{BBC-LP+F} attempted, the fractional configuration
added $60{,}627{,}660$ cuts, and the configuration without fractional separation added
none, which is the expected control.

They do not help. Table~\ref{tab:frac} gives the comparison.

\midrule
\multicolumn{3}{@{}l}{final dual bound, comparing the two on the $1{,}238$ instances where both are recorded:}\\

\caption{The effect of enabling fractional Benders cuts. Sixty million cuts enter the

This is a clean negative result and it is worth stating plainly. The mechanism works:
the cuts are valid, they are generated, and they enter the master. What they do not do
is raise the bound. On the instances where the coverage bound is loose --- the
instances where a stronger bound would be worth having --- the fractional dual
information adds essentially nothing to what the integer cuts already provide.

The pattern is consistent and it is the same pattern as the fractional cuts. The only
strengthening that helps is the one that supplies a better \emph{incumbent}. Every
attempt to improve the \emph{bound} costs more than it returns. The conflict-graph row
is neutral, which is what Section~\ref{ssec:accel} predicted for it on structural
grounds: on most instances the coverage row already dominates it.

\paragraph{Where the search stops.}
Two further measurements locate the difficulty precisely.

First, of the $699$ instances \texttt{BBC-LP} solves, $255$ are closed at the root
node, without branching at all.

Second, consider the runs that hit the time limit. There are $593$ of them. For $272$
of those the optimum is known, because some other method proved it. On $194$ of those
$272$, which is $71\%$, the Benders solver's incumbent already \emph{equals} that
optimum. It had found the answer and could not prove it.

Taken together: when the bound is good the solver finishes at the root, and when the
bound is bad it finds the optimum quickly and then spends an hour failing to certify
it. The search is not the bottleneck. The bound is.

\subsection{The coverage bound decides the outcome}\label{ssec:results-tight}

Section~\ref{sec:gap} predicted that the coverage bound $q$ would separate easy
instances from hard ones for any configuration-based method. That prediction is
directly testable, because for each instance with a known optimum it can be checked
whether the optimum equals the bound.

Of the $974$ instances whose optimum is known, $494$ have the coverage bound tight and
$480$ do not. The split is almost even, at $50.7\%$, which means the standard instance
collections are not concentrated in either regime.

Splitting the Benders solver's results by that criterion gives
Table~\ref{tab:tightness}.

\begin{table}[h]\centering
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
& instances & solved by \texttt{BBC-LP} & solved \\
\midrule
coverage bound tight ($\Zst=q$)  & $494$ & $465$ & $94\%$\\
coverage bound loose ($\Zst>q$)  & $480$ & $234$ & $49\%$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The Benders solver's success rate, split by whether the coverage bound equals
the optimum. The bound, not the size of the instance, is what decides the outcome.}
\label{tab:tightness}
\end{table}

This is the central measurement of the campaign. The same solver, on the same
hardware, under the same time limit, closes $94\%$ of one class and $49\%$ of the
other. The classification is not by number of jobs, number of tools or density; it is
by a single structural property of the instance, and it is the property the theory of
Section~\ref{sec:gap} identifies.

\subsection{Branch-and-price}\label{ssec:results-bnp}

The two position-indexed prototypes, PCF$'$ and PTF, were implemented, validated
against brute-force optima on small instances, and run under the protocol used for the
earlier interim campaign. Their runs under the uniform protocol of Section~\ref{ssec:bench} are in progress at
the time of writing, so no results from them appear in this section.

What can be stated without those runs is structural, and it is the more important part.
Proposition~\ref{prop:pcf} shows that the position--configuration relaxation equals the
coverage bound $q$, so a branch-and-price built on it inherits exactly the limitation
that Table~\ref{tab:tightness} measures for the Benders solver.
Proposition~\ref{prop:ptf} shows that the position--transition relaxation can exceed
$q$, which makes it the one construction in this report that is not so limited.
Establishing by how much, and on which instances, is the natural next experiment.

The comparison with the Job Grouping Problem is informative here.
\citet{Otiai2024} builds a branch-and-price for that problem whose set-covering
relaxation is strong enough to close benchmark instances in a handful of nodes. For the
SSP, \citet{Moreira2026} reports that the trivial bound $\lvert T\rvert-b$ obtained by
the multicommodity model was the best available bound on all thirty instances of three
of his subgroups, with the configuration model giving the best bound on four instances
of the remaining, tighter subgroup. The configuration relaxation for the SSP is
therefore not, in general, an improvement on the counting bound, which is the same
conclusion Proposition~\ref{prop:pcf} reaches analytically.

\subsection{What the campaign establishes}\label{ssec:results-summary}

Five statements are supported by the measurements above.

\begin{enumerate}[topsep=4pt,itemsep=3pt]
  \item The reimplemented multicommodity model of \citet{daSilva2024} is the strongest
  single method in this study, which replicates the claim of its authors on an
  independent implementation.

  \item Cross-method agreement is complete. On all $974$ instances solved by more than
  one method, no two methods disagree on the cost of the schedule returned.

  \item Fractional Benders cuts, once the implementation defect is corrected, are
  generated in very large numbers and are counterproductive. They raise the dual bound
  on three instances out of $1{,}238$ and cost sixty-nine solved instances.

  \item Of four strengthenings of the Benders solver, only the one that improves the
  incumbent helps. The three that aim at the bound are neutral or harmful.

  \item Whether the coverage bound equals the optimum decides whether the Benders
  solver closes the instance: $94\%$ against $49\%$. Where it fails, the incumbent is
  already optimal $71\%$ of the time.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
The first two are checks that the study is sound. The last three all say the same
thing, which Section~\ref{sec:disc} takes up.

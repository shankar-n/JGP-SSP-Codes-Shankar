\section{The problem}

\begin{frame}{A machine, a magazine, and a set of jobs}
\vspace{0.12cm}
\begingroup\centering
\input{_fig_setup}
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A machine can hold only $b$ tools at a time. Each job needs its own subset.
  \item Running the jobs in some order means \textbf{stopping to change tools}, and each
        stop costs production time.
  \item \textbf{The problem.} Choose the order of the jobs, and the tools loaded at each
        step, so that the total number of tool insertions is smallest.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{The order decides how many tools come back}
\vspace{0.09cm}
\begingroup\centering
\scalebox{.90}{\input{_fig_twoorders}}
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.27cm}
Same three jobs, same magazine, same loading rule. Only the order changed.
Running job~2 in the middle keeps its tool loaded; running it last forces that tool to
be \textbf{removed and inserted again}.
\vspace{0.15cm}

\textbf{That second insertion is called a reinsertion.} Everything in this talk is about
where reinsertions come from and why they are hard to rule out.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{One decision is easy, the other is not}
\vspace{0.21cm}
\textbf{Loading is easy.} Once the order is fixed, the cheapest loading is known in
closed form: when a tool must enter a full magazine, remove the one whose next use lies
furthest ahead. This is the \emph{Keep Tool Needed Soonest} rule, it is optimal, and it
runs in $O(n\lvert T\rvert)$ time \textbf{(Tang \& Denardo, 1988)}.

\vspace{0.27cm}
\textbf{Ordering is hard.} The problem is NP-hard for every fixed magazine capacity
$b\ge2$ \textbf{(Crama et al., 1994)}. A good order puts jobs with similar requirements
next to one another --- but ``similar'' means only \emph{overlapping sets}, with no
natural distance, and the capacity couples jobs that are far apart in the order.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{block}{Why this shape matters}
An \textbf{easy inner problem inside a hard outer one} is exactly what decomposition
methods are built for. That is the reason this problem has attracted exact methods for
four decades, and it is the starting point of the second half of this talk.
\end{block}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{How hard is the ordering, concretely?}
\vspace{0.12cm}
\begingroup\centering
\includegraphics[height=.60\textheight,width=.92\linewidth,keepaspectratio]{assets/report_figures/landscape.pdf}
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.12cm}
Every one of the $40{,}320$ orderings of one eight-job benchmark instance, by cost.
The optimum is $13$ and only $180$ orderings attain it. The elementary counting bound
$q$ is $10$ --- three below the optimum, and no ordering ever reaches it.
\end{frame}


\section{State of the art, and what was open}

\begin{frame}{Where the exact methods stop}
\vspace{0.12cm}
\begingroup\centering
\scalebox{.72}{\input{_fig_gtsp}}
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.24cm}
{\small Compact integer models have been proposed for four decades
\textbf{(Laporte et al., 2004; Catanzaro et al., 2015)}. The strongest of those surveyed
here relaxes to $q=\lvert\U\rvert-b$: the number of tools used minus the magazine
capacity \textbf{(da Silva et al., 2021)}.}

\vspace{0.15cm}
{\small That is an \textbf{elementary counting argument} --- every tool must enter at
least once, and at most $b$ can be there before the first job. A solver relying on it
cannot prove optimality where that count is wrong.}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{What practice does, and what was not known about it}
\vspace{0.18cm}
\textbf{Industry does not solve this problem directly.} It groups the jobs into as few
magazine-fitting batches as possible, then orders the batches. It is fast, and where it
has been measured against exact optima in industrial printing it does well
\textbf{(Burger et al., 2015)}.

\vspace{0.27cm}
The construction is known to be a $b$-approximation
\textbf{(Crama \& van de Klundert, 1999)}. What had \emph{not} been done:
\begin{itemize}
  \item sharpen that ratio to something that depends on the instance in front of you;
  \item say on which instances the construction is \textbf{exact};
  \item distinguish the two different values the method can be read as returning.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{0.18cm}
The third turns out to change the first two.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Two questions, and why only these two}
\vspace{0.21cm}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{.46\textwidth}
\textbf{Question 1 --- structural}\\[.25cm]
{\small What does insisting on the \emph{fewest} groups cost, once the final loading
step is allowed to re-optimise?\\[.3cm]
Answering it needs no solver: it is a question about the construction itself.}

\column{.46\textwidth}
\textbf{Question 2 --- algorithmic}\\[.25cm]
{\small Every compact model surveyed stops at the counting bound $q$. Can a method that
\emph{never forms} that relaxation get past it?\\[.3cm]
Two classical routes qualify, for opposite reasons.}
\end{columns}

\vspace{0.36cm}
\begin{block}{Why these two and no others}
The first is the gap the literature left open about the method industry actually uses.
The second follows from the problem's shape --- an easy loading problem inside a hard
ordering problem --- which is precisely the precondition for \textbf{decomposition}.
Everything else in the exact literature already sits below $q$.
\end{block}
\end{frame}
\section{Structural results}

\begin{frame}{The construction admits two different readings}
\vspace{0.09cm}
\begingroup\centering
\scalebox{.55}{\input{_fig_frames}}
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.21cm}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{.46\textwidth}
\textbf{(a) One magazine per group.}\\[.15cm]
{\small The reading the literature's worst cases are built on.}
\column{.46\textwidth}
\textbf{(b) Re-optimise at the end.}\\[.15cm]
{\small What the method as specified actually returns.}
\end{columns}

\vspace{0.22cm}
\begingroup\centering\noindent
On the family most often used as an example, (a) pays one extra insertion and (b) pays
\textbf{none}. Separating the two is a prerequisite for saying anything correct here.\par\endgroup
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{What the structural half established}
\vspace{0.18cm}
\textbf{The loss has a single cause.} Allowing \emph{all} feasible groupings makes the
construction exact --- the optimum equals the cheapest walk over every grouping. So the
loss is not a defect of the method; it is the price of insisting on the fewest groups.

\vspace{0.24cm}
\textbf{Guarantees that depend on the instance.} A ratio bound
$\min(b,\Kst-1,q)$ holding uniformly over every admissible grouping and tie-breaking
order, families where the construction is provably exact for every capacity, and the
extremal behaviour settled at three groups.

\vspace{0.24cm}
\textbf{The headline.} There is a \textbf{connected} family with
\[
\lvert\U\rvert=8g+1,\qquad \Kst=3g,\qquad \Zst=8g-5,\qquad H=9g-5,
\]
so the additive loss is $g$ and grows without bound. The \emph{ratio} tends to $9/8$,
so a constant factor is not disproved.

\vspace{0.21cm}
\centering\small
This answers the question left open by \textbf{(Crama \& van de Klundert, 1999)} for the
method as it is actually specified.
\end{frame}
\section{A branch-and-Benders-cut algorithm}

\begin{frame}{The decomposition}
\vspace{0.15cm}
\begingroup\centering
\scalebox{.84}{\input{_fig_benders}}
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.27cm}
The master chooses a job order using an optimistic stand-in $\theta$ for the loading
cost. The subproblem returns the \textbf{exact} loading cost of that order. If $\theta$
undershot, one cut lifts it --- and that cut is valid for \emph{every} order, not only
the current one.

\vspace{0.18cm}
Because the subproblem is polynomial, the master's bound is assembled from true
subproblem values, never from a relaxation of the loading --- the logic-based
\textbf{(Hooker \& Ottosson, 2003)} and combinatorial \textbf{(Codato \& Fischetti, 2006)}
Benders lineage.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{The master problem}
\vspace{0.18cm}
Introduce a depot $d$ with no requirement and arc variables $x_{ij}\in\rset{0,1}$, so
the $x$ describe a Hamiltonian path through the jobs.
\vspace{0.09cm}
\[
\min\;\theta \qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad
\sum_{i\ne j}x_{ij}=1\ \ (\forall j),\qquad
\sum_{j\ne i}x_{ij}=1\ \ (\forall i),
\]
\[
\sum_{i,j\in S}x_{ij}\le\lvert S\rvert-1 \quad (S\subseteq J).
\]

\vspace{0.21cm}
\textbf{Two bounds the master starts from.} The \emph{coverage row}
$\theta\ge\lvert\U\rvert$, and a \emph{pairwise row}
\[
\theta\ \ge\ \sum_{i,j\in J}w_{ij}x_{ij}+\sum_{j\in J}\lvert T_j\rvert x_{dj},
\qquad w_{ij}=\max\bigl(0,\lvert T_i\cup T_j\rvert-b\bigr),
\]
counting what two adjacent jobs must differ by. Both are visible to the linear
relaxation; keep that in mind for later.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{The subproblem: loading for a fixed order}
\vspace{0.12cm}
Fix a candidate order $\bar x$. Let $y_{j,t}=1$ if tool $t$ sits in the magazine while
job $j$ runs, and let $z_{j,t}$ charge an insertion of $t$ at $j$.
\vspace{0.06cm}
{\small
\[
\begin{aligned}
\min\;\; & \textstyle\sum_{j,t} z_{j,t} && \\
 & y_{j,t}\ \ge\ 1 && t\in T_j &&(\nu_{jt})\\
 & \textstyle\sum_t y_{j,t}\ \le\ b && \forall j &&(\mu_j)\\
 & z_{j,t}\ \ge\ y_{j,t}-y_{i,t}-(1-\bar x_{ij}) && \forall i\ne j,\ t &&(\lambda_{ijt})\\
 & z_{j,t}\ \ge\ y_{j,t}-(1-\bar x_{dj}) && \forall j,\ t &&(\lambda^{d}_{jt})
\end{aligned}
\]}
\vspace{-0.09cm}
The third row charges an insertion when a tool is present at $j$ but absent at its
predecessor $i$. The fourth does the same for the first job, charging its whole
requirement, \textbf{because the machine starts empty}.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Why the oracle is exact, and not a price}
\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{block}{Integrality}
For fixed $\bar x$ the master variables appear only on the right-hand side, so what
remains is a linear program in $y$ and $z$ alone. Its constraint matrix has the
\textbf{consecutive-ones property} along the path $\bar x$ selects, and is therefore
totally unimodular. The relaxation is integral, and its optimum is exactly
$\KTNS(\bar x)$ \textbf{(Tang \& Denardo, 1988)}.
\end{block}

\vspace{0.36cm}
\textbf{Why this matters for the whole method.} A Benders scheme is only as good as its
subproblem. Here the subproblem is not approximated and not relaxed --- it returns the
true cost of the proposed sequence. So when the solver holds a good incumbent, that
incumbent has been evaluated \emph{exactly}.

\vspace{0.21cm}
What remains in question is whether the master accumulates a strong enough
\textbf{lower bound} to prove no better order exists. That is the rest of this talk.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{The optimality cut, in full}
\vspace{0.18cm}
Only $\bar x$ appears on the right-hand side, so in the dual it appears only in the
objective. Taking an optimal dual solution
$(\bar\lambda,\bar\lambda^{d},\bar\mu,\bar\nu)$:
\vspace{0.15cm}
\[
\boxed{\;\;
\theta\;\ge\;
\sum_{i\ne j}\sum_{t}\bar\lambda_{ijt}\,(x_{ij}-1)
\;+\;\sum_{j}\sum_{t}\bar\lambda^{d}_{jt}\,(x_{dj}-1)
\;-\;b\sum_{j}\bar\mu_j
\;+\;\sum_{j}\sum_{t\in T_j}\bar\nu_{jt}\;\;}
\]

\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{.46\textwidth}
\textbf{Valid for every order.}\\[.15cm]
{\small The dual feasible region does not depend on $\bar x$, so the same cut holds at
every point of the master.}
\column{.46\textwidth}
\textbf{Tight at this one.}\\[.15cm]
{\small At $\bar x$ it reproduces the exact loading cost. It is affine in $x$, so
separating it at fractional points needs no extra argument.}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{A defect found and corrected}
\vspace{0.27cm}
\begin{block}{The depot term was initially omitted}
Dropping $\sum_{j,t}\bar\lambda^{d}_{jt}(x_{dj}-1)$ makes the cut \textbf{too tight}: it
silently assumes the first job's tools are free, and it cuts off genuine optima.
\end{block}

\vspace{0.33cm}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{.46\textwidth}
\textbf{How it surfaced}\\[.2cm]
{\small Random instances were solved twice --- once by brute force, once by the solver.
The two disagreed on \textbf{193} of them, always with the solver's answer too high.}
\column{.46\textwidth}
\textbf{What was done}\\[.2cm]
{\small The term was restored, the derivation was rewritten from the implementation
rather than from the draft, and the \textbf{entire campaign was re-run}.}
\end{columns}

\vspace{0.33cm}
\centering\small
This is why the validation described later runs against an \emph{independent}
implementation, not against the solver itself.
\end{frame}
\section{Strengthening the solver}

\begin{frame}{Four strengthenings, each a standard remedy}
\vspace{0.24cm}
All four are classical elements of the Benders toolbox surveyed by
\textbf{(Rahmaniani et al., 2017)}. Each is an independent switch, checked against brute
force \emph{before} any campaign run, so a strengthening can never silently return a
wrong optimum.

\vspace{0.33cm}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item \textbf{Fractional Benders cuts} --- separate the same cut at fractional points,
        not only at integer orders.
  \item \textbf{A strong incumbent at the root} --- a hybrid genetic search, used both as
        a warm start and to seed a cut.
  \item \textbf{A conflict-graph bound} --- a combinatorial row the linear relaxation
        cannot see.
  \item \textbf{Pareto-optimal cut lifting} --- when the dual has many optima, choose the
        deepest cut.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.27cm}
\centering
Two attack the \textbf{dual bound}. One attacks the \textbf{incumbent}. One chooses
\emph{among} cuts. Watch which kind helps.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Fractional Benders cuts}
\vspace{0.21cm}
\textbf{The intention.} An integer-only scheme learns about the loading cost only once
an order is fully committed. A fractional cut extracts the same dual information from a
\emph{partial} order and removes it from the relaxation at once. This is the standard
remedy for tailing-off, and it is legitimate here because the cut is affine in $x$.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{.46\textwidth}
\textbf{It did what it should, locally}\\[.25cm]
{\small $67.5$ million cuts generated.\\[.2cm]
Median node count fell from $\mathbf{77{,}439}$ to $\mathbf{3{,}701}$ --- a factor of
twenty.}

\column{.48\textwidth}
\textbf{And the bound never moved}\\[.25cm]
{\small Across $1{,}404$ paired runs the dual bound at termination was\\[.2cm]
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
higher & \textbf{0}\\
equal  & $1{,}226$\\
lower  & $178$\\
\end{tabular}\\[.25cm]
and \textbf{69} instances were lost.}
\end{columns}

\vspace{0.27cm}
\centering
The remedy is being applied correctly, to the wrong obstacle.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{A strong incumbent at the root}
\vspace{0.21cm}
\textbf{The intention.} A branch-and-bound search prunes in proportion to the quality of
its incumbent. A compact form of the hybrid genetic search of
\textbf{(Mecler et al., 2021)} is run once, at the root.

\vspace{0.24cm}
{\small
Constructive seeds --- nearest-neighbour orders on the pairwise weights $w_{ij}$, and a
largest-tool-first order --- each improved by a variable-neighbourhood local search using
adjacent swaps and short segment relocations, every candidate scored by the exact
loading oracle, evolved under order crossover with a diversity guard.}

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textbf{The order is then used twice.} As a warm start it gives the solver an incumbent
from node zero. And it seeds a cut: the loading dual is solved once for the heuristic
order and its cut added before the first relaxation --- the initial-cut-pool acceleration
of \textbf{(Rahmaniani et al., 2017)}.

\vspace{0.24cm}
\centering
This is the only strengthening aimed at the \textbf{primal} side, and the only one that
helps: $\mathbf{+31}$ instances.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{A conflict-graph bound}
\vspace{0.21cm}
\textbf{The intention.} The master's root bound is pairwise plus coverage, and both are
visible to the linear relaxation. The combinatorial structure they miss lives in the
\emph{conflict graph} $G$: vertices are jobs, edges join pairs that cannot share a
magazine.

\vspace{0.24cm}
A clique of $G$ is a set of pairwise incompatible jobs, which must occupy that many
distinct configurations, so $\Kst\ge\omega(G)$; more generally $\Kst\ge\chi(G)$, and
where $G$ is non-bipartite $\chi(G)\ge3$ even when $\omega(G)=2$. The resulting row
\[
\theta\ \ge\ (\chi_{\mathrm{lb}}-1)+\min(b,\lvert\U\rvert)
\]
is added whenever it strictly exceeds the coverage row. This is the simplest of the
conflict-graph inequalities studied by \textbf{(Atamt\"urk et al., 2000)}.

\vspace{0.24cm}
\textbf{Its reach is narrow by construction.} On most instances the coverage row already
dominates it. It can bite only where the \emph{grouping} structure, rather than the tool
count, forces the switches. Measured effect: $-1$ instance.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Pareto-optimal cut lifting}
\vspace{0.21cm}
\textbf{The intention.} When the loading dual has several optimal solutions --- which a
degenerate transportation-type problem routinely does --- the cuts they yield are not
equally strong. Choosing the deepest is the Pareto-optimal cut of
\textbf{(Magnanti \& Wong, 1981)}: among alternative dual optima, select the one
maximising the cut's value at a fixed interior core point of the master.

\vspace{0.24cm}
\textbf{(Papadakos, 2008)} makes this practical by observing that the core point need not
be tied to the current relaxation, and may simply be updated as a convex combination of
the points visited. That form is used here: a core point is kept in the relative
interior, initialised from the heuristic order blended with the barycentre, and drawn
halfway towards the current relaxation point after each separation.

\vspace{0.27cm}
The core-point cut is added \emph{in addition to}, never instead of, the ordinary cut,
which keeps the scheme valid by construction.

\vspace{0.21cm}
\centering
Measured effect: $\mathbf{-37}$ instances --- a deeper cut inside the wrong family.
\end{frame}
\section{Computational results}

\begin{frame}{Experimental design}
\vspace{0.24cm}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{.44\textwidth}
\textbf{What was compared}\\[.25cm]
{\small Nine Benders regimes and three reimplemented compact baselines: the tool-state
model \textbf{(Laporte et al., 2004)}, Formulation~4 \textbf{(Catanzaro et al., 2015)},
and the multicommodity model \textbf{(da Silva et al., 2021)}.}

\column{.50\textwidth}
\textbf{Under one protocol}\\[.25cm]
{\small $1{,}410$ canonical instances $\times$ $12$ configurations $=16{,}920$ planned
outcomes, all present. One hour per run, one homogeneous node type, and a
\emph{fixed} denominator: every planned pair counts once, and any non-optimal outcome
counts as not closed.}
\end{columns}

\vspace{0.36cm}
\begin{block}{Two caveats stated before the numbers}
Benders ran on one solver thread while the compact models ran on four; and F4 stands in
for the stronger Formulation~5 that its authors recommend. Both run \emph{against} this
report's own conclusion, not for it.
\end{block}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{How far each method gets}
\vspace{0.12cm}
\begingroup\centering

\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.15cm}
Against the same $1{,}410$-instance denominator the compact baselines certify more than
every Benders regime. \textbf{This does not go the way I hoped}, and with the two caveats
above the margin is if anything understated.

\vspace{0.12cm}
\centering
The rest of this section is about \emph{why}, and the reason is not the search.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Solving times}
\vspace{0.12cm}
\begingroup\centering

\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.15cm}
Restricted to the $531$ instances every method solves, the picture inverts: the Benders

\vspace{0.12cm}
So this is not a slow method. It closes an instance almost at once, or it does not close

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{What separates the two cases}
\vspace{0.12cm}
\begingroup\centering
\includegraphics[height=.48\textheight,width=.84\linewidth,keepaspectratio]{assets/report_figures/tightsplit.pdf}
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.12cm}
{\small Group the $1{,}107$ instances of known optimum by how far the optimum sits above
the counting bound. Where the bound is exact the Benders solver certifies $94\%$; one
unit above, $31\%$; then no trend at all.}
\vspace{0.09cm}

{\small \textbf{But this is a fact about the method, not the instances.} F4 moves the
\emph{other} way and closes all $45$ of the loosest. And the multicommodity model's
relaxation is also $q$, yet it loses only $13$ points. What loses $54$ is a master
carrying the coverage row and, at the root, almost nothing else.}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{And the failure is a certificate failure}
\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{block}{The solver usually already has the answer}
Among time-limited runs whose optimum is known from elsewhere, the Benders incumbent
\textbf{already equals that optimum} in $223$ of $383$ cases. The median remaining gap is
nonetheless $23.1\%$.
\end{block}

\vspace{0.33cm}
Put beside the ablation, the pattern is consistent:

\vspace{0.18cm}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item the one strengthening aimed at the \textbf{incumbent} gains instances;
  \item every strengthening aimed at the \textbf{dual bound} is neutral or harmful;
  \item $67.5$ million fractional cuts raise the bound at termination on \emph{no}
        instance.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.27cm}
\centering
A solver whose difficulty was \emph{finding} good orders would not behave like this.
\end{frame}


\section{Why the bound does not move}

\begin{frame}{The cost is not a local quantity}
\vspace{0.24cm}
Write $\beta_t$ for the number of maximal stretches during which tool $t$ sits in the
magazine. Every insertion of $t$ begins one, so
\[
\Zst=\sum_{t\in\U}\beta_t,
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\beta_t\ge1 \text{ for every used tool}
\]
recovers exactly the counting bound $q$. \textbf{Everything above $q$ is a reinsertion}:
a tool evicted and brought back.

\vspace{0.27cm}
A reinsertion happens when the jobs needing a tool are pushed apart by other jobs whose
requirements cannot sit alongside it. That is a property of the \textbf{global
interleaving} of the schedule. It is not a property of which pairs of jobs happen to be
adjacent.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{And every inequality the solver carries is local}
\vspace{0.12cm}
\begingroup\centering
\includegraphics[height=.48\textheight,width=.83\linewidth,keepaspectratio]{assets/report_figures/rootgap.pdf}
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.12cm}
{\small The master carries one row indexed by \emph{arcs}, triplet rows that are the weak
linearisation of a product, and a constant coverage row. Every optimality cut it generates
is also arc-supported, with coefficient $\sum_t\lambda_{ijt}$ on $x_{ij}$.}

\vspace{0.12cm}
{\small So the master learns only \textbf{adjacency-indexed} facts about a cost that is not
adjacency-indexed. Measured: on $87.2\%$ of runs the root relaxation equals the coverage
row exactly --- the pairwise and triplet rows contribute nothing at all.}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{A family that is not local, and what it was worth}
\vspace{0.21cm}
For a stretch of positions $W=[p,r]$, every tool needed inside $W$ is either already
loaded at $p-1$ or inserted within $W$:
\[
\sum_{t}\sum_{k\in W} w_{t,k}\ \ \ge\ \ \bigl\lvert\{t \text{ needed in } W\}\bigr\rvert
\;-\;\sum_{t} a_{t,p-1}.
\]
Valid, and it counts across a \emph{span} rather than an arc. Its ceiling over integral
schedules is several times that of the family the solvers carry today.

\vspace{0.27cm}
\begin{block}{And it delivered nothing}
Implemented and measured inside the master, it left the root relaxation unchanged on all
$37$ instances for which both values were obtained --- because its fractional row turns
out to be \textbf{implied} by rows the formulation already has.
\end{block}

\vspace{0.18cm}
\centering
Writing a global inequality down is necessary. It is not sufficient.
\end{frame}
\section{Limitations}

\begin{frame}{What this study does not show}
\vspace{0.21cm}
Ordered by how much each threatens the conclusions just drawn.

\vspace{0.24cm}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
  \item \textbf{The census of heuristic gaps is small.} The one fixed random census is
        $1{,}260$ instances at three to five jobs. The connected family settles
        \emph{existence} and unboundedness; it says nothing about how often such gaps
        occur at industrial sizes.
  \item \textbf{A ceiling over integral schedules is not a bound.} The window family's
        headroom was measured by enumerating every sequence of $62$ loose cases at eight
        jobs. That is a screen, not an estimate --- and the implemented row raised no
        root.
  \item \textbf{The benchmark directory was not the published benchmark.} It held $171$
        files against the $160$ defined; ten duplicate canonical cases byte for byte and
        one is a smoke test. All eleven are filtered explicitly, and the count is
        checked before any figure is computed.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What this study does not show, continued}
\vspace{0.21cm}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
  \item \textbf{One hour, one machine, one collection --- and F4, not F5.} Every solve
        count comes from a single limit on one node type, and the compact baseline is
        weaker than the best available. Both cut \emph{against} the conclusion drawn
        here, and neither touches the relaxation values.
  \item \textbf{The branch-and-price prototypes are Python; the baselines are C.} A
        pricing node costs orders of magnitude more than a node inside a commercial
        solver. Nothing above nine jobs was closed, and that carries no information
        about the formulations --- which is why no conclusion here rests on a
        branch-and-price solve count, only on a root value recorded before any search.
  \item \textbf{The learned cut-selection prototype was never run on this problem.} It
        was trained on knapsack cover cuts. It shows that the agent learns; it says
        nothing about the SSP, and it is reported as an appendix for that reason.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\section{Conclusions and further work}

\begin{frame}{What was established}
\vspace{0.15cm}
{\small One equivalence organises the whole report: a schedule is a \textbf{walk through
magazine configurations}, and its cost is the length of that walk.}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{.47\textwidth}
\textbf{Structural}\\[.18cm]
{\footnotesize
The two-phase construction has two readings; separating them is a prerequisite for
saying anything correct about it.\\[.14cm]
The optimum is the cheapest walk over \emph{all} groupings --- the gap is exactly the
price of minimum cardinality.\\[.14cm]
A ratio bound $\min(b,\Kst-1,q)$ holds for \emph{every} instance, and zero-gap classes
exist at every capacity.\\[.14cm]
For the method as specified: four jobs are necessary and sufficient for a positive gap,
and a \emph{connected} family proves it unbounded.}

\column{.47\textwidth}
\textbf{Algorithmic}\\[.18cm]
{\footnotesize
A branch-and-Benders-cut solver whose subproblem is exact, polynomial, and never
relaxed.\\[.14cm]
Position-indexed formulations with a fixed polynomial row set, one of which
\emph{provably} exceeds the coverage bound.\\[.14cm]
Across $16{,}920$ outcomes, four independent measurements agree: the obstacle is the
\textbf{bound}, not the search.\\[.14cm]
Bound-limited, not implementation-limited --- a negative result about a family of
methods, not one solver.}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Further work}
\vspace{0.21cm}
\begin{block}{The direction the campaign points to hardest}
A bound that reaches into the loose half. The arc-supported information is exhausted;
the honest next step is a global family whose \emph{fractional} encoding is not implied
by rows the formulation already has --- which is exactly where the window family failed.
\end{block}

\vspace{0.35cm}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep7pt
  \item \textbf{Two concrete strengthenings, both untested.} Chain PTF per configuration
        rather than per tool --- that is what confines its advantage to two runs. And
        disaggregate the master's single $\theta$ into one variable per position, the
        standard remedy the ablation never tried.
  \item \textbf{The exact worst case of the heuristic as specified.} Settled at three
        groups for the walk value; open for the method itself. Two concrete questions:
        is the gap ever larger than one on a connected instance, and does
        $\mathrm{gap}\le\Kst-2$ hold? Directed searches at $b\in\{3,4,5\}$ found no
        counterexample to either.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Further work, continued}
\vspace{0.45cm}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep10pt
  \item \textbf{Non-uniform tool costs, for free.} The subproblem is the loading linear
        program, not the KTNS rule, and that program stays exact when tools have
        different setup times while the rule does not \textbf{(Crama et al., 1994)}. The
        solver therefore extends unchanged to a regime the whole SSP literature assumes
        away, and testing it there would broaden the method's scope.
  \item \textbf{Beyond the SSP.} Nothing in the position--transition construction is
        specific to tool switching except the transition metric; it applies to any
        generalised travelling-salesman problem with overlapping clusters
        \textbf{(Pop et al., 2024)}. Whether the relaxation strength observed here
        survives that generality is open.
  \item \textbf{Where a learned component could pay.} Not cut selection --- a selector
        cannot select a cut that does not exist. Column selection for the position-indexed
        pricers, whose coupled-pair oracle is the measured bottleneck, is the target with
        a chance of helping.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup --- Branch-and-price: why reformulate at all}
\vspace{0.27cm}
\begin{block}{The obstacle}
Every compact model surveyed relaxes to the counting bound $q=\lvert\U\rvert-b$ or
below. A branch-and-cut built on any of them is fast where $q$ is exact and adrift
where it is not.
\end{block}

\vspace{0.3cm}
Reformulating over \textbf{magazine configurations} attacks this from the other side.
A configuration is a set $C\subseteq\U$ with $\lvert C\rvert=b$; these become the
columns, generated on demand. Three formulations were built.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Column} & \textbf{Linear relaxation}\\
\midrule
PCF        & configuration at a position & $=q$ with the counting rows, $0$ without\\
PCF$'$     & prefix form of PCF          & $=q$, symmetry removed\\
PTF        & \emph{transition} between two & $\ \ge q$, and can exceed it\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Backup --- PCF: configurations at positions}
\vspace{0.18cm}
$y_C^{k}=1$ if configuration $C$ occupies position $k$; $w_t^{k}\ge0$ counts an
insertion of $t$ at position $k$.
\vspace{0.15cm}
{\small
\[
\begin{aligned}
\min\;\; & \textstyle\sum_{t,k}w_t^{k} &&\\
\text{(P)}\;\; & \textstyle\sum_C y_C^{k}=1, & k&=1,\dots,n\\
\text{(Cov)}\; & \textstyle\sum_k\sum_{C\supseteq T_j}y_C^{k}\ \ge\ 1, & j&\in J\\
\text{(W)}\;\; & w_t^{k}\ \ge\ \textstyle\sum_{C\ni t}\bigl(y_C^{k}-y_C^{k-1}\bigr), & t&\in T,\ k\ge2\\
\text{(T)}\;\; & \textstyle\sum_{k\ge2}w_t^{k}+\sum_{C\ni t}y_C^{1}\ \ge\ 1, & t&\in\U
\end{aligned}
\]}
\vspace{-0.06cm}
\begin{block}{The counting rows are load-bearing}
Without (T) the relaxation is \textbf{zero} --- one convex blend of configurations,
repeated at every position, satisfies (P), (Cov) and (W) at no cost. With (T) it is
exactly $q$. \emph{Proved, and confirmed on all $2{,}108$ recorded roots.}
\end{block}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Backup --- PTF: transitions as columns}
\vspace{0.18cm}
$z_{C,C'}^{k}\ge0$ is a column for the step from position $k$ to $k+1$ leaving $C$ and
entering $C'$, at cost $d(C,C')=\lvert C'\setminus C\rvert$.
\vspace{0.12cm}
{\small
\[
\begin{aligned}
\min\;\; & \textstyle\sum_{k}\sum_{(C,C')}d(C,C')\,z_{C,C'}^{k} &&\\
\text{(S)}\;\; & \textstyle\sum_{(C,C')}z_{C,C'}^{k}=1, & k&=1,\dots,n-1\\
\text{(F)}\;\; & \textstyle\sum_{(C,C'):t\in C'}z^{k}_{C,C'}=\sum_{(D,D'):t\in D}z^{k+1}_{D,D'}, & t&\in T\\
\text{(Cov)}\; & \text{every job served at the head or tail of some step} &&\\
\text{(T)}\;\; & \textstyle\sum_{k}\sum_{(C,C'):t\notin C,\,t\in C'}z^{k}_{C,C'}+\sum_{(C,C'):t\in C}z^{1}_{C,C'}\ \ge\ 1, & t&\in\U
\end{aligned}
\]}
\vspace{-0.06cm}
\begin{block}{Why this one can beat the counting bound}
A column \emph{carries a step}, so the relaxation cannot mix configurations without
paying the transition. Hence $\mathrm{LP}(\mathrm{PTF})\ge q$, and it can be strictly
greater --- the only construction in this work that provably exceeds $q$.
\end{block}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Backup --- pricing, and what the prototypes measured}
\vspace{0.24cm}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{.46\textwidth}
\textbf{The pricing problem}\\[.25cm]
{\small Choosing the entering configuration is \textbf{NP-hard}: pick $b$ tools to
collect per-tool duals plus a bonus for each job fully covered. PTF prices
\emph{coupled pairs}, so a node costs far more again.}
\vspace{0.24cm}

\textbf{Rows stay small}\\[.25cm]
{\small $O(n\lvert T\rvert)$ and fixed; only the columns are generated.}

\column{.48\textwidth}
\begin{block}{What was measured}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt\small
  \item PCF$'$ root $=q$ on \textbf{all $2{,}108$} recorded runs --- the proposition
  confirmed on the benchmark.
  \item PTF exceeds $q$ on \textbf{2 of 232} roots, rising from $5$ to $6$ where the
  optima are $12$ and $13$.
  \item Nothing above nine jobs was closed: a Python pricer against compiled solvers.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}
\vspace{0.15cm}
{\small Sound, and as it stands too rare and too small to close the certificate gap.}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup --- a learned policy for choosing cuts}
\vspace{0.21cm}
{\small\textbf{The question, raised by Prof.~Colares.} At a node the master has several
admissible cuts: one per candidate order evaluated, the coverage row, and one per
violated fractional point. A fixed rule decides. Would a learned policy do better?}

\vspace{0.2cm}
{\small A prototype was built following the cut-selection framework of
\textbf{(Tang et al., 2020)}: selection as a Markov decision process --- state is the
relaxation point plus a candidate pool summarised by violation, support size, right-hand
side and mean coefficient; reward is the rise in the dual bound; the policy is a linear
softmax trained by REINFORCE.}

\vspace{0.2cm}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{.44\textwidth}
{\footnotesize Trained and evaluated on \textbf{knapsack cover cuts}, not on the SSP:
the Benders solver needs a commercial solver the development environment did not have.
$2{,}000$ episodes at each of six sizes, one seed each, held-out evaluation.}

\column{.50\textwidth}
\centering\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrr@{}}
\toprule
$n$ & learned & random & gain\\
\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{columns}

\vspace{0.2cm}
\centering
{\small Two things follow and only two: \textbf{the machinery learns}, and that is a
statement \textbf{about knapsack cover cuts}. An appendix, not a method.}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Backup --- and why running it here would not have helped}
\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{block}{The reason is the same one the whole talk turns on}
Cut selection chooses \emph{among cuts a method can already generate}. Its established
role in the literature is to pick well, not to make a family stronger.
\end{block}

\vspace{0.3cm}
In this campaign the available selections did not improve the terminal bound on a single
instance --- $67.5$ million fractional cuts raised it on none. A selector cannot select a
strong cut that does not exist.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textbf{Where learning would be worth trying instead.} Column selection for the
position-indexed pricers, whose coupled-pair oracle is the measured bottleneck. The
non-learned version of the same idea is automatic dual stabilisation.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup --- how the numbers were made trustworthy}
\vspace{0.25cm}
\small
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt

        them do the two values differ.
  \item \textbf{A fixed, audited denominator.} Every planned canonical pair counts once,
        certified or not; any non-optimal outcome counts as not closed. $16{,}920$
        planned outcomes, all present.
  \item \textbf{The identity of a run was tightened.} The historical runner keyed on file
        name and configuration alone, so repeated Crama names left $58$ planned pairs
        unrecorded; they were completed under the same protocol before aggregation. The
        key is now (family, file, jobs, tools, capacity, configuration).
  \item \textbf{A predicted identity, confirmed on real data.} Empty-start and
        free-initial costs must differ by the initial load. SSPMF reports the latter; the
        difference equals $b$ on $1{,}019$ of the $1{,}028$ instances it closed --- and is
        smaller on exactly the nine whose optimum opens with a job needing $<b$ tools.
  \item \textbf{Every figure is regenerated from the raw ledger} by a checking script,
        and $58$ independent mathematical checks re-run from the source data.
\end{itemize}

{\small The counts would move, and they would not move equally for all methods --- which
is why the comparison is reported at one fixed limit and the conclusions rest on
relaxation values rather than on solve counts.}

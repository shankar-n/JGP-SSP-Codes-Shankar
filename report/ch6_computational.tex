%==============================================================================
\section{Computational study}\label{sec:comp}
%==============================================================================

Computation here is how the theory is tested. The quantity the
structural results single out, the coverage bound $q$, is also the root relaxation of
every configuration-based method built here except PTF, which exceeds it on two runs
of $232$, so a benchmark that records optima and bounds
measures the theory's central variable directly. The campaign also settles a question
that predates the theory and motivated the Benders solver in the first place: the SSP
has a polynomially solvable loading subproblem, which is the textbook precondition for
Benders decomposition, and whether a decomposition can exploit it against modern
compact models had not been tested.

\paragraph{What the section asks, and in what order.}
Each subsection states the question it answers before the measurement that answers it.
Section~\ref{ssec:bench} fixes the protocol and Section~\ref{ssec:validation} asks
whether the implementations are sound. Section~\ref{ssec:results-solved} asks how far
each method gets; Section~\ref{ssec:results-bbc} whether the difficulty is finding good
solutions or proving them optimal, and whether the standard strengthenings move it;
Section~\ref{ssec:results-tight} what separates the instances a method closes from
those it does not. Section~\ref{ssec:results-bnp} puts the same question to the
branch-and-price prototypes, which report their relaxation value directly and so answer
it without reference to solving times. Section~\ref{sec:disc} then asks why the answer
is what it is.

\subsection{Experimental design}\label{ssec:bench}

\paragraph{Instances.}
The benchmark uses the standard SSP families of \citet{daSilva2024},
\citet{Crama1994} and \citet{Laporte2004}. A source audit after the campaign found that
the checked-in Catanzaro directory contained $171$ files rather than the published
$160$: \texttt{A1-1C} through \texttt{A1-10C} are byte-for-byte duplicates of
\texttt{A2-1} through \texttt{A2-10}, and \texttt{A0-0} is an ad-hoc test with no
benchmark provenance. The canonical analysis excludes those eleven file identities.
Table~\ref{tab:families} therefore contains $1{,}410$ instances.

The protocol targeted all $1{,}421$ checked-in files under all twelve
configurations, or $17{,}052$ planned pairs, and the completed ledger contains one
outcome for each pair. Of those,
$132$ rows belong to the eleven non-canonical Catanzaro files and are retained in the
raw archive but excluded from every aggregate below. This leaves all $16{,}920$
canonical planned pairs. The fixed-denominator tables count every non-optimal outcome
as unsolved, so no observation is selected by outcome. Section~\ref{ssec:validation}
and Appendix~\ref{app:repro} give the completion and provenance checks.
Table~\ref{tab:families} gives the sizes.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
Every run of the Benders solver and of the compact baselines has the same one-hour
limit and compute allocation. There is no early stopping and no
per-configuration retirement rule. Apart from the explicit configuration switches and
thread settings described below, the remaining solver parameters are left at their
defaults. The branch-and-price prototypes are run separately, under a thirty-minute limit
on the Catanzaro and Crama families and ten minutes on the Laporte families, for the
reason given in Section~\ref{ssec:results-bnp}.
An auxiliary LSS sensitivity run used a different resource allocation and is never
merged into this primary protocol; Appendix~\ref{app:repro} records it separately.

\paragraph{Hardware and software.}
All runs were executed on the LIMOS SLURM cluster, confined to a homogeneous pool of
identical nodes (AMD EPYC~7452, $2\times32$ cores, $512$\,GB of memory). The scheduler
allocated four CPU cores to every primary run and exported a four-thread setting.
The compact baselines honour that setting, but the Benders runner instantiates its
solver with parallel mode disabled and the implementation consequently pins the CPLEX
master to one thread. Thus comparisons within the nine Benders configurations use a
common effective thread count; cross-method solve counts and wall times describe the
tested implementations under equal allocation, not a comparison at equal effective
CPLEX threads. The mixed-integer solver is CPLEX~22.1.1 throughout. The branch-and-price
prototypes run single-threaded under PySCIPOpt. The campaign was distributed over $120$ SLURM
array shards and contains $17{,}052$ raw result rows, of which $16{,}920$ enter the
canonical analysis.

\paragraph{Configurations.}
Twelve solver configurations were run. Three are the reimplemented compact baselines:
F4 of \citet{Catanzaro2015}, written \texttt{CATZ-F4}; LSS of \citet{Laporte2004}; and
SSPMF of \citet{daSilva2024}. F4 was chosen over the F5 that \citet{Catanzaro2015}
recommend because it is the smaller of the two models that share the F3 relaxation, and
implementing F5 did not fit in the time available. The compact side of this comparison
is therefore weaker than the literature allows, and every statement made from it is
made in that knowledge; Section~\ref{ssec:results-summary} reports what follows, which
is that the baselines win anyway. The remaining nine are the Benders solver, in an ablation
over its cut families and its strengthenings. The naming is as follows:
\texttt{BBC-LP} uses the dual optimality cuts, \texttt{BBC-K} the combinatorial cuts,
\texttt{+T} adds the triplet bounds, \texttt{+F} enables fractional separation,
\texttt{+C} the conflict-graph row, \texttt{+H} the hybrid-genetic incumbent,
\texttt{+P} the Pareto-optimal cut lifting, and \texttt{+ACC} all three strengthenings
together.

Two implementation details limit causal readings of this ablation. Enabling
\texttt{+F} also disables CPLEX presolve, because the fractional user-cut callback is
not usable on the presolved model in this implementation. Enabling \texttt{+H} runs
the default two-second HGS before the recorded CPLEX timer and supplies both a MIP
start and one seeded Benders cut. The rows below therefore compare complete measured
configurations; they are not perfectly isolated estimates of a single feature's
effect.

\paragraph{The reported quantity.}
The common metric is the empty-start cost of the sequence a method returns, evaluated
by the loading oracle. Each formulation has its own native objective, and two of them
use the free-initial convention, so the native values are not comparable across
methods. The empty-start cost of the returned sequence is defined for every method
regardless of its formulation, and it is what makes the cross-method comparison of
Section~\ref{ssec:validation} meaningful.

\subsection{Validation}\label{ssec:validation}

The implementations were subjected to independent computational checks before any
result was recorded, and again from the results themselves. No amount of such checking
proves correctness; what follows is what was checked and what it found.

\paragraph{Before the campaign.}
Every exact solver was checked against brute-force optima on a bank of small instances,
in every combination of its option flags. The loading oracle itself was verified
against an exact dynamic program over magazine states; the verification described in
Appendix~\ref{app:verify} repeats this on $349{,}872$ instance-and-sequence pairs and
finds no disagreement.

\paragraph{Identifying an instance.}
An instance is identified by the family it comes from, its file name, its number of
jobs, its number of tools, and its magazine capacity. All five are needed. The Crama
collection reuses the same file names at four different capacities, so two runs that
agree on the name can be runs on different instances. Keying on the name alone merges
rows describing different instances and produces apparent disagreements between methods
where none exists. The comparison below uses the full identifier.

The runner must use the same identity, together with the solver configuration, when it
resumes a shard. The historical campaign version used only file name and configuration,
so repeated names left $58$ planned pairs unrecorded. They were completed under the same
protocol before aggregation. The repository runner now uses (family, file name, jobs,
tools, capacity, configuration) for every outcome, and a regression check exercises the
repeated Crama names at all four capacities.

\paragraph{Cross-method agreement.}
Of the $1{,}410$ canonical instances, $1{,}107$ were solved to proven optimality by at least one
method and $988$ by at least two. On those $988$ there are $41{,}514$ pairs of methods
that both proved an optimum. \emph{In none of them do the two disagree.} Every pair of
methods that both closed an instance returned a sequence of the same empty-start cost.

\paragraph{The two conventions, measured.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:conv} states that the empty-start and free-initial costs of a
sequence differ by the size of the initial load. The campaign confirms it on real
instances. The SSPMF model reports the free-initial cost natively, and on all
$1{,}028$ instances it closed, the difference between its reported value and the
empty-start cost of the same sequence equals the magazine capacity $b$ on $1{,}019$ of
them. On the other nine it is smaller than $b$, and those are exactly the instances on
which an optimal sequence begins with a job that needs fewer than $b$ tools, so the
magazine is not filled at the start. The two baselines that report the empty-start cost
natively, \texttt{CATZ-F4} and LSS, show a difference of zero on all $1{,}709$ runs in
which they closed an instance.

\paragraph{Tools that no job needs.}
Four matrices in the collection contain a tool that appears in no job's requirement
set: Crama \texttt{s2n007} and \texttt{s2n009}, Laporte3 \texttt{L28-7} and Laporte5
\texttt{L6-8}. Counted with the capacities at which they appear, that is ten of the
$1{,}410$ instances. The coverage bound of Corollary~\ref{cor:lb} must therefore be read as
$q=\lvert\U\rvert-b$, using the set $\U$ of tools that some job actually requires, and
not as $\lvert T\rvert-b$ using the tool count declared in the instance file. On those
ten the two differ, and the smaller value is the correct one: on Laporte3
instance \texttt{L28-7}, which declares $25$ tools of which one is never required and
has $b=5$, the optimum in the free-initial convention is $19$, which is
$\lvert\U\rvert-b=24-5$ and not $\lvert T\rvert-b=20$. All coverage-bound figures in
this section use $\lvert\U\rvert$.

\paragraph{A defect found and corrected.}
The Benders master carries the coverage row $\theta\ge\lvert\U\rvert$ of
Section~\ref{ssec:bbc} as an explicit constraint. It has to: unlike every other method
here, the master has no formulation of the loading problem from which that bound would
follow, so without the row it is not available to the relaxation at all.
Observation~\ref{obs:rootq} shows how much work the row does --- on $87.2\%$ of canonical runs the
root relaxation is exactly $\lvert\U\rvert$, so no other row improves its value.

\paragraph{Fixed-denominator accounting.}
Every planned canonical identity contributes exactly once. A certified optimum counts
as solved; every valid non-optimal termination counts as unsolved. The completion audit
identified entries that required completion or replacement, reran them under the same
protocol, and validated the merged ledger before any aggregate was computed. Thus no
canonical identity is absent. Appendix~\ref{app:repro} records the detailed provenance
and keeps the auxiliary LSS sensitivity ledger separate.

establishes a ranking, and that ranking is the starting point for everything after it,
but by itself it says nothing about \emph{why} the ranking is what it is.
Sections~\ref{ssec:results-bbc} and \ref{ssec:results-tight} take that up.

The shapes differ, and the difference is the point. \texttt{BBC-LP} closes $402$
instances in the first second and $724$ in the full hour, so more than half of
everything it will ever solve is solved in that first second. SSPMF starts lower
relative to its own total, at $474$ of $1{,}028$, and LSS lower still, at $56$ of
$833$. What the front-loading does \emph{not} mean is that the Benders curve has

therefore move every count here, and would move them unequally --- the one method close
to saturation is SSPMF, which gains $78$ over the same stretch.

The Benders solver is not slow, then, and it is not out of time either. It is fast on
one class of instance and, on the other, still grinding when the hour ends, which is
the behaviour Section~\ref{ssec:results-tight} explains and
Section~\ref{ssec:results-bbc} confirms from the size of the certificate gap it is left
holding.

\paragraph{How far from proven the unsolved runs are.}
The complement of the solved count is the gap remaining when the limit is reached,
measured between the incumbent and the dual bound. For \texttt{BBC-LP} the median over
its $680$ time-limited runs is $23.1\%$ and the mean is $29.0\%$, with a maximum of
$73.1\%$; the nine Benders configurations lie in the same broad range on the mean.
Only $50$ of those $680$ runs finish within five per cent of proven.

That figure should be read against the one in the next paragraph. On the runs where the
optimum is known independently, the solver's incumbent already equals it $58\%$ of the
time. It is therefore sitting on the right answer with a certified gap of roughly a
quarter, and the hour is spent failing to close a gap that exists only in the
certificate.

\subsection{The Benders solver and its strengthenings}\label{ssec:results-bbc}

\emph{The question.} A branch-and-bound method can fail for two reasons: it cannot find
a good solution, or it finds one and cannot prove it optimal. The two call for opposite
remedies. Four strengthening regimes were implemented, one aimed primarily at the
incumbent and three at the dual bound. Their measured pattern can distinguish the two
explanations only suggestively, because \texttt{+F} also changes presolve and
\texttt{+H} supplies both a start and a cut outside the recorded CPLEX timer. It was
nevertheless not obvious in advance which complete regime would perform best.

\paragraph{Fractional cuts.}
Separating Benders cuts at fractional points is a standard acceleration whose value on
this problem had not been established. The measurement below settles it.

The cuts are now generated in very large numbers. Across the $1{,}410$ instances that
both \texttt{BBC-LP} and \texttt{BBC-LP+F} attempted, the fractional configuration
added $67{,}047{,}595$ cuts, and the configuration without fractional separation added
none, which is the expected control.

The implemented fractional regime does not help under this budget. Recall that this
regime also disables presolve; Table~\ref{tab:frac} compares the two complete settings.

The mechanism works:
the cuts are valid, they are generated, and they enter the master. The node counts show
they do tighten the relaxation locally, since the search explores twenty times fewer
nodes. What they do not do is raise the bound that matters. On no instance in the
comparison is the bound at termination higher with them than without.

One reading is that the fractional dual information is largely redundant with what the
integer cuts already give the master, while separation consumes the budget. The
simultaneous presolve change means this experiment does not identify how much of the
loss is caused by the cuts alone; it establishes the performance of the implemented
regime.

\paragraph{Where the search stops.}
Two further measurements locate the difficulty precisely.

First, of the $724$ instances \texttt{BBC-LP} solves, $260$ are closed at the root
node, without branching at all. The root relaxation value was recorded on $1{,}174$ of
the $1{,}410$ runs, and on $877$ of those the optimum is also known. On those $877$ the
root value equals the optimum $291$ times, and the split is sharp: among the runs that
went on to close the instance it is already the optimum in $261$ of $494$, and among
the runs that did not close it in only $30$ of $383$. Where it is not the optimum it is
short of it by at least one unit and by $16.7\%$ at the median, rising to $42.9\%$.

Second, consider the runs that hit the time limit. Across the nine Benders
configurations there are $6{,}575$ of them, and for $3{,}872$ the optimum is known
because some method proved it. On $2{,}529$ of those $3{,}872$, which is $65\%$, the
Benders solver's incumbent already \emph{equals} that optimum. It had found the answer
and could not prove it. For \texttt{BBC-LP} alone the figure is $223$ of $383$, or
$58\%$.

Taken together these measurements identify certification as the dominant bottleneck on
many tested Benders runs. They do not show that search is never limiting: not every
tight-bound case closes at the root, and not every loose-case incumbent is optimal.

\subsection{How coverage tightness separates closure, and for which methods}\label{ssec:results-tight}

Section~\ref{sec:gap} suggests that the coverage bound $q$ should separate outcomes
most sharply for a method whose dual information largely reduces to that bound. That
prediction is directly testable, because for each instance with a known optimum it can
be checked whether the optimum equals the bound.

Of the $1{,}107$ instances whose optimum is known, $520$ have the coverage bound tight
and $587$ do not. The split is close to even, at $47.0\%$ tight, which means the
standard instance collections are not concentrated in either regime. Where the bound is
loose it is loose by a wide margin: the optimum exceeds it by $5.81$ on average and by
as much as $30$.

This is the central measurement of the campaign. The same solver, on the same hardware,
under the same time limit, closes $94\%$ of one class and $40\%$ of the other. The
classification is not by number of jobs, number of tools or density; it is by a single
structural property of the instance, and it is the property the theory of
Section~\ref{sec:gap} identifies.

The other three columns say what that measurement is and is not about, and the
distinction matters enough to state carefully. SSPMF is the near control: its relaxation
also equals $q$ when every tool is used, so if the bound were the whole story it should
collapse too. It does not --- it falls from $100\%$ to $87\%$ where the Benders solver
falls from $94\%$ to $40\%$ --- but it does fall, and it falls hardest exactly where the
Benders solver does, at the loosest end.

\texttt{CATZ-F4} is the control that refutes the stronger reading. Its relaxation does
not equal $q$ and is in general below it (Table~\ref{tab:methods}), and its curve does
not track the bound at all: it closes $71\%$ of the tight instances and $86\%$ of the
loose ones, and every one of the $45$ where the bound is loosest.

So the three columns are an ordering rather than a dichotomy, and the ordering is what
the subsection can support. Ranked by how much of what the method knows at a node is
the coverage bound \emph{and nothing else}, the loss across the split is $-54$ points
for the Benders master, which carries $q$ as an explicit row and, by
Observation~\ref{obs:rootq}, nothing else on $87.2\%$ of canonical runs; $-13$ for SSPMF, whose
relaxation also equals $q$ where every tool is used but which hands its search a full
compact description of the problem at every node; and $+15$ for \texttt{CATZ-F4}, whose
relaxation is not $q$ at all. Stated carefully: \emph{on this benchmark, tightness of
the coverage bound is the strongest measured separator of closure for a method whose
dual information reduces to that bound, and the association weakens as the method
carries more.} It is not a statement that loose instances are hard --- the row above
rules that out --- and it is an association, not a demonstrated cause.

What separates \texttt{CATZ-F4} from both is not a better starting bound but a
formulation whose bound can move once the solver branches. That is a reading, not a
measurement: nothing here records how \texttt{CATZ-F4}'s dual bound evolves inside
CPLEX, and testing it would need the node-level trace that the campaign does not
collect. It is recorded because it is the reading that
Section~\ref{ssec:disc-locality} then makes precise, and because the alternative
reading --- that these instances are simply hard --- is ruled out by the row above.

\subsection{Branch-and-price: the relaxation measured directly}\label{ssec:results-bnp}

\emph{The question.} Everything above measures the bound indirectly, through how many
instances a solver closes. The prototypes can answer directly, because they report the
value of their root relaxation on every run. Two propositions of Section~\ref{ssec:pos}
predict what that value will be --- that PCF$'$ equals the coverage bound and that PTF
can exceed it --- and the campaign is a chance to test a proof against a benchmark
rather than against another proof.

That is the whole purpose of this run, and it is worth being explicit about what it is
not. It is not a fourth entry in the comparison of Section~\ref{ssec:results-solved}.
The prototypes are implemented in Python over PySCIPOpt with a Python pricer, so a
single node costs orders of magnitude more than a node of a compiled solver; their
wall-clock times measure the implementation language, not the formulation, and are
never compared against CPLEX anywhere in this report. The quantity this subsection
exists to report is the root relaxation value, which is a property of the formulation
and is recorded before any search happens. The solve counts are given because a
reader will ask for them, and are read only as evidence that the prototypes are
working code.

In total $2{,}574$ canonical runs were made over $468$ instances in seven
configurations: PCF$'$ alone; PCF$'$ with each of the four accelerations of
Section~\ref{ssec:pos} separately, written \texttt{+HP} for heuristic pricing,
\texttt{+MC} for multiple pricing, \texttt{+WS} for warm starting and \texttt{+STAB}
for dual stabilisation; PCF$'$ with all four together, written \texttt{+ACC}; and PTF.
The time limit is thirty minutes on the Catanzaro and Crama families and ten minutes on
the Laporte families.

Two properties of this run make its counts weaker still, and both are stated before the
numbers. First, the harness carries an early-stop rule: once a configuration has failed
to close six increasingly hard instances in succession, the remaining harder instances
are skipped for that configuration. The number of runs a configuration received
therefore depends on how it performed, so the denominators in Table~\ref{tab:bnp} are
not comparable across rows. Second, no repetition or seed variation was run. Every
comparison drawn below is made either on instances that both configurations attempted,
or on the root relaxation value, which the early-stop rule cannot bias because it is
recorded before any search.

\paragraph{They agree with the campaign.}
On the $500$ runs that proved an optimum and where the same instance was also closed in
the campaign of Section~\ref{ssec:results-solved}, the two agree on every one. As in
Section~\ref{ssec:validation}, the native objective is the free-initial cost and the
difference from the empty-start cost is exactly $b$ on all $500$.

\paragraph{What the root relaxation shows.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:pcf} states that the position--configuration relaxation equals
the coverage bound $q$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:ptf} states that the
position--transition relaxation is at least $q$. Strict excess is the finite
Observation~\ref{obs:ptf-strict}. Both are testable directly, because the
prototypes record the root relaxation value.

\emph{PCF$'$ never exceeds $q$.} On all $2{,}108$ PCF$'$ runs for which a root
relaxation value was recorded, that value equals $\lvert\U\rvert-b$ exactly. Not one
run, in any of the six PCF$'$ configurations, produced a root bound above the counting
bound. The proposition is proved independently of the campaign; what the campaign adds
is that no implementation defect masks it on $2{,}108$ runs.

\begin{observation}[PTF exceeds coverage, but rarely]\label{obs:ptf-strict}
Of the $232$ canonical PTF runs with a recorded root relaxation, two are strictly above
$\lvert\U\rvert-b$: Laporte3 \texttt{L11-6} and \texttt{L11-7}, where the bound is $6$
against $q=5$. The other $230$ runs sit exactly on $q$, and on those two instances the
optima are $12$ and $13$, so a bound of $6$ instead of $5$ closes a small part of a
large gap.
\end{observation}

\emph{The bound is often already the answer.} On $990$ of the $1{,}839$ runs where both
the root relaxation and the optimum are known, the root relaxation \emph{equals} the
optimum. These are the tight instances of Section~\ref{ssec:results-tight}, seen from
the other side: where the coverage bound is tight, a configuration-based relaxation
needs no strengthening at all, and where it is loose, neither PCF$'$ nor PTF supplies
one.

Table~\ref{tab:bnp} gives the counts, for completeness rather than for comparison.
Nothing above nine jobs was closed by any configuration: all $608$ runs on the $30$-job
and $40$-job instances reached the time limit. This is the expected consequence of a
Python pricer and is not evidence about the formulation, which the paragraph above has
already measured.

Head to head on the $319$ instances both attempted, PCF$'$ and PTF each close $72$.
Their node counts differ by a factor of fifty --- a median of $96$ against $2$ ---

\paragraph{The comparison with the Job Grouping Problem.}
\citet{Otiai2026} builds a branch-and-price for that problem whose set-covering
relaxation closes the ten reported instances with small search trees: seven use at most
five nodes, and the other three use $15$, $21$ and $79$. For the
SSP, \citet{Moreira2026} reports that the trivial bound $\lvert T\rvert-b$ obtained by
the multicommodity model was the best available bound on all thirty instances of three
of his subgroups, with the configuration model giving the best bound on four instances
of the remaining, tighter subgroup. The measurements above say the same thing on a
larger collection and with the mechanism visible: the configuration relaxation for the
SSP is not, in general, an improvement on the counting bound.

\subsection{What the campaign establishes}\label{ssec:results-summary}

Two of the results above are checks that the study is sound. Cross-method agreement is
complete: on the $988$ instances solved by more than one method, all $41{,}514$ pairs
agree on the cost of the schedule returned. And the three reimplemented compact models
all close substantially more instances than any configuration built here. On the fixed
$1{,}410$-instance denominator, their certified ordering is SSPMF,
\texttt{CATZ-F4}, then LSS.

The remaining four converge on one statement. Whether the coverage bound equals the
optimum is strongly associated with whether the tested Benders solver closes an instance ---
a fact about that solver rather than about the instance, since \texttt{CATZ-F4} closes
all $45$ of the instances where the bound is loosest --- and where it fails the
incumbent is usually already optimal. Among the measured strengthening regimes, only
\texttt{+H} improves the certified count; it primarily targets the incumbent but also
adds a seed cut and uncharged root preprocessing. The tested bound-oriented regimes
are neutral or harmful under the budget. The implemented fractional regime is the
extreme case: it adds sixty-seven million cuts, also disables presolve, raises the
terminal bound on no paired instance, and certifies sixty-nine fewer. Both branch-and-price
relaxations sit on the coverage bound or barely above it, confirming
Propositions~\ref{prop:pcf} and \ref{prop:ptf} and showing the second to be of little
practical use as it stands. Section~\ref{sec:disc} takes that up.

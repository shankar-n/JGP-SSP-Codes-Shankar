JGP--SSP INTERNSHIP DEFENCE: SPEAKER SCRIPT

Main talk: slides 1-20, target 23.5-25 minutes including transitions.
Backup material: slides 21-23, used only in questions.

Speak to the picture or plot; do not read every label.


SLIDE 1 -- TITLE

Good morning. This project is about a machine with a limited tool magazine. Each job
needs a set of tools, and the job order decides which tools have to be loaded again.
I studied this in two ways: first, the loss created by grouping compatible jobs; and
second, an exact Benders approach for the full sequencing problem. The common theme
is reinsertion: a tool was loaded, removed, and is needed again later.

I will start with a small example, then the grouping result, and finally the exact
campaign.


SLIDE 2 -- THE ORDER DECIDES WHICH TOOLS RETURN

The magazine has capacity b. Before a job starts, every required tool must be inside.
Missing tools are inserted and, if the magazine is full, another tool must leave.

Here, the same three jobs are run in two orders. In the first order, a useful tool is
kept until its next use. In the second, it is evicted and must return. The requirements
are identical; only the order changed, but the insertion count changed with it.

This is the Shortest Sequence Problem, or SSP: choose the job order and magazine
states to minimise insertions. The hard part is preserving a useful tool across the
jobs in between.


SLIDE 3 -- LOADING IS EASY; THE ORDER IS THE HARD PART

For a fixed job order, the loading problem is easy. KTNS means Keep Tool Needed
Soonest. When a tool must leave, remove one that is not required now and whose next
use is farthest in the future. Tang and Denardo proved that this rule is optimal. It
runs in O of n times the number of used tools.

Choosing the order is the hard part. SSP is NP-hard for every fixed capacity of at
least two. This separation suggests decomposition: the master chooses the order, and
an exact loading oracle evaluates it.


SLIDE 4 -- TWO GAPS THE LITERATURE LEAVES OPEN

The exact SSP literature mainly tries to strengthen the ordering lower bound. LSS is
an early compact formulation. Catanzaro and co-authors proposed several later compact
formulations; I use their Formulation 4 as a reference baseline. SSPMF is a
multicommodity formulation whose relaxation equals the basic coverage bound in the
relevant setting.

There are stronger and newer methods, including Catanzaro Formulation 5, JGSMF, and
dynamic-programming or A-star approaches. They are not baselines in this campaign. So
the computational part is a controlled comparison with three reimplemented reference
models, not a state-of-the-art ranking.


SLIDE 5 -- GROUPING COMMITS BEFORE IT SEES THE SEQUENCE

The grouping approach first partitions jobs into groups whose combined tool set fits
in one magazine. It uses as few groups as possible. It then orders the groups, expands
each group into jobs, and runs KTNS on the final sequence.

It is attractive because each group can be processed without switching tools inside
the group. It also has a classical approximation guarantee. But that guarantee does
not answer a more specific question: after insisting on the minimum number of groups,
how much extra switching can the final KTNS schedule suffer?

The next slides answer that question exactly for a connected family.


SLIDE 6 -- DROP THE FEWEST-GROUPS RULE: GROUPING IS EXACT

Let U be the tools used anywhere, and assume the initial magazine can be filled for
free. At most b tools fit initially, so at least q equals the size of U minus b tools
must be inserted later. This is the coverage bound.

Every schedule has a simple accounting identity: cost equals q plus R, where R counts
reinsertions. First appearances contribute to coverage. Any cost above coverage is a
tool returning after it was removed.

For the grouping pipeline, H is the best KTNS cost over every minimum grouping, every
group order, and every order inside a group. This gives the heuristic every favourable
tie-break. If H still exceeds the SSP optimum Z star, the gap belongs to grouping
itself rather than to a poor implementation choice.


SLIDE 7 -- FOUR JOBS ARE ENOUGH FOR A GENUINE GROUPING LOSS

This small instance has capacity four. Jobs A and B are the only pair that can share a
group. Any minimum grouping therefore forces A and B to be adjacent in the final
sequence.

Without that restriction, the order B, C, A, D reaches the coverage bound: three
insertions after the free initial fill. With the grouping restriction, checking the
six grouped orders shows that the best cost is four.

Thus Z star equals three and H equals four. One forced adjacency creates one
reinsertion that a free sequence avoids.


SLIDE 8 -- ONE SHARED TOOL MAKES THE FAMILY CONNECTED

To make a family, take g private copies of the four-job seed. Add one universal tool
to every job, add one private marker to each copy, and raise capacity to six.

The universal tool connects all copies: every job shares it. The marker keeps copies
distinct and prevents small jobs from different copies being packed together. The local
obstruction from the seed survives, but the whole instance is connected.

Inside one copy, the two small jobs A and B are the only feasible pair. Each large job
fills the magazine, and small jobs from different copies need seven tools. The minimum
grouping is therefore unique and contains three groups per copy, so K star equals
three g.


SLIDE 9 -- THE UNRESTRICTED ORDER REACHES COVERAGE

The family has eight private tools per copy and one universal tool. With capacity six,
the coverage bound is q equals eight g minus five.

Process copies one after another, using B, C, A, D inside every copy. Keep the
universal tool loaded. The remaining slots reproduce the zero-reinsertion order of the
seed. When moving to the next copy, every private tool is new, so no old tool returns.

The first copy contributes three paid insertions and each later copy contributes eight.
The schedule reaches q, so Z star equals eight g minus five.


SLIDE 10 -- EVERY MINIMUM GROUPING FORCES ONE RETURN PER COPY

In every minimum grouping, A and B must be consecutive. The universal tool and marker
already occupy two of the six slots, leaving the same four-slot obstruction as in the
seed.

Slide 7 showed that no order with A and B adjacent can reach coverage in that
four-slot seed. Therefore each copy forces at least one tool to return. A matching
grouped schedule pays exactly one return per copy, so H equals q plus g, or nine g
minus five.


SLIDE 11 -- THE ADDITIVE GAP GROWS WITHOUT BOUND

The plot puts both exact formulas together. The unrestricted optimum is eight g minus
five. The grouped value is nine g minus five. Their vertical distance is exactly g.

That proves an unbounded additive loss on connected instances. The ratio tends to nine
eighths. This construction therefore does not prove a universal ratio bound; it proves
the narrower and fully checked statement that the absolute number of avoidable
reinsertions can grow without limit.


SLIDE 12 -- ORDER IN THE MASTER, LOADING IN AN EXACT ORACLE

I now turn to the exact approach. The master proposes a job order. The loading oracle
evaluates that exact order. If the master's variable theta understates the loading
cost, the oracle returns a cut. The master is then solved again with that cut.

This is Benders decomposition in its simplest form: choose a path in the master, price
that path exactly in the subproblem, and feed a global lower bound back. The cut must
be valid for every future order, not only for the order that generated it.

The next slides show where that validity comes from, and why such cuts can still be too
local to describe a long reinsertion span well.


SLIDE 13 -- THE MASTER STARTS FROM LOCAL AND COVERAGE BOUNDS

The master uses binary arc variables x sub i j. One means that job j follows job i.
Degree constraints and subtour constraints make these arcs one path through all jobs.
Theta is the loading-cost lower bound.

Before Benders cuts, theta has coverage and local adjacency information. The pair row
charges switching forced by consecutive jobs. There is also a coverage row, and one
regime adds triplet rows.

Subtours are separated at integer candidates, which is sufficient for integer path
feasibility. Fractional subtour cuts were not implemented or tested. Root-bound
results later are therefore about this implemented relaxation, not the full fractional
subtour polytope.


SLIDE 14 -- THE LOADING LP IS INTEGRAL ON A FIXED PATH

For a fixed path, the subproblem decides whether each tool is loaded at each job and
whether it is inserted on each transition. Requirement constraints keep current-job
tools present; capacity constraints limit the magazine to b tools.

This linear program is integral for a fixed order. Its objective equals the KTNS cost
of that order. So the master is not receiving a heuristic price. It receives the exact
cost of its proposed sequence.

This matters for the results: when Benders has a good incumbent, the sequence itself
has been evaluated exactly. The remaining question is whether the master has learned a
strong enough lower bound to prove that no better sequence exists.


SLIDE 15 -- THE DUAL CUT IS SUPPORTED ONLY ON ARCS

The dual of the loading problem assigns prices to requirement, capacity, and
transition constraints. From an optimal dual solution for the current path, we collect
transition prices into coefficients alpha sub i j.

This gives a cut of the form theta greater than or equal to beta plus the sum of alpha
sub i j times x sub i j. It is tight at the current path and valid for every path,
because the dual feasible region does not depend on the selected path.

The issue is not validity. The cut sees selected adjacencies well, but a reinsertion
can depend on a tool disappearing and returning much later. The campaign tests several
ways of strengthening this mechanism.


SLIDE 16 -- THE CAMPAIGN TESTS 12 SOLVER CONFIGURATIONS

The campaign has nine Benders regimes and three compact reference models. BBC-LP uses
dual cuts at integer solutions. BBC-K uses a KTNS-based cut. Other regimes add triplet
rows, fractional separation, a conflict row, a hybrid start, or Pareto lifting.

There are 1,410 canonical instances and 12 configurations, so 16,920 planned rows.
All are present. Each run has a one-hour limit under one fixed resource envelope.
Costs are rechecked with one common empty-start KTNS oracle.

Two fairness caveats matter. BBC ran with one CPLEX thread while compact baselines ran
with four. The fractional-cut setting disables presolve, and the hybrid setting
includes both a MIP start and a seeded cut before the CPLEX timer. The comparison is
complete, but it is not thread-balanced or one-factor-at-a-time.


SLIDE 17 -- EVERY COMPACT BASELINE CERTIFIES MORE THAN BENDERS

This is the headline comparison, and it does not go my way. Against the same 1,410-instance
denominator, SSPMF certifies 1,028 optima, Catanzaro F4 876, and LSS 833. The strongest
Benders regime, BBC-LP, reaches 724.

I want to be precise about what that does and does not show. It is evidence about the
implemented methods under this protocol. BBC ran on one CPLEX thread while the compact
models ran on four, and Catanzaro F4 stands in for the stronger Formulation 5 that its
authors recommend. So the margin is real, and if anything it understates the baselines.

The point of the rest of this section is that the reason for the gap is visible, and it is
not the search.

Transition: the next slide shows that when Benders does close an instance, it closes it fast.


SLIDE 18 -- BENDERS IS QUICK, WITH A LONG TAIL

Restricting to the 531 instances that every method solves, the picture inverts. The Benders
boxes sit far to the left: BBC-K has a median around a hundredth of a second, against tens of
seconds for the compact models. The axis is logarithmic.

So this is not a slow method. It is a method that either closes an instance almost at once or
does not close it inside the hour. That shape is the signature of a bound that is either
exactly right or not useful, rather than of a search that is grinding.

Transition: so what decides which of the two happens?


SLIDE 19 -- CLOSURE DROPS WHEN THE BOUND MISSES THE OPTIMUM

Group the 1,107 instances of known optimum by how far the optimum sits above the coverage
bound. Where coverage is exact, BBC-LP certifies 94 percent. One unit above, it falls to
31 percent, and then it wanders with no trend: once the bound is loose at all, by how much
stops mattering.

Two guards on that reading. First, it is a fact about this master, not about the instances:
Catanzaro F4 moves the other way and closes every one of the 45 instances where the bound is
loosest. Second, it is not even true of every method whose relaxation is the coverage bound.
SSPMF's relaxation is also q, and it loses only thirteen points across the same split. What
loses fifty-four is a master carrying coverage and, at the root, almost nothing else --
87.2 percent of recorded Benders roots sit exactly on that row.

And the failure is a certificate failure, not a discovery failure. Among time-limited BBC-LP
runs whose optimum is known from elsewhere, 223 of 383 already hold that optimum.

Transition: that tells us what a better method has to do.


SLIDE 20 -- NEXT: PRICING TOOL RESIDENCY ACROSS A SPAN

Here is the through-line. Minimum grouping can force a tool out and back, so the cost is the
coverage count plus reinsertions. Every inequality either method carries is attached to a
single arc or a single position, and a reinsertion is created across a span. That is the
mismatch.

I tested the obvious repair. A window family that counts tools across a stretch of positions
is valid over integral schedules and has a much larger ceiling. But its fractional row turns
out to be implied by rows the formulation already has, and it raised none of the 37 roots
measured. Being able to write a global inequality down is necessary; it is not sufficient.

So the next experiments are: disaggregate theta by position; encode job-union structure over a
window; and test any stronger bound under a matched protocol. Separately, the grouping result
is usable directly -- search groupings of size K star up to K star plus delta, flatten each
with KTNS, and use the orders as seeds.

The work narrows the next experiment. It does not claim a new state-of-the-art solver.


BACKUP SLIDE 21 -- CERTIFIED OPTIMA BY METHOD AND FAMILY

The per-family table behind slide 17. Denominators are 160, 160, 340, 330, 340 and 80,
totalling 1,410 for every row, so the columns are directly comparable. Laporte 3 and 4 are

BACKUP SLIDE 22 -- FRACTIONAL CUTS SPEND MORE AND CERTIFY LESS

The ablation, with BBC-LP plus fractional separation as the reference at 655 certified optima.
Removing fractional separation gives plain BBC-LP and adds 69. Adding the hybrid incumbent
adds 31. The conflict row costs one, all three strengthenings together cost three, and Pareto
lifting costs 37.

Fractional separation does what it is supposed to do locally: it cuts the median node count
from 77,439 to 3,701, using about 67 million cuts. Yet across 1,404 paired terminal bounds it
is higher zero times, equal 1,226 times, and lower 178 times. More cuts did not buy more
certificates.

One caveat: the fractional setting also disables presolve and the hybrid setting receives a
seeded cut, so these are whole-regime comparisons rather than isolated causal effects.


BACKUP SLIDE 23 -- PROOF AND VALIDATION LEDGER

For the connected family: let x sub i be the number of seed tools from copy i in the free
initial magazine, and y the number of initially loaded universal or marker tools. Capacity
gives the sum of the x sub i plus y at most six. The seed forces one reinsertion per copy, so
counting first appearances and those returns gives at least nine g minus five insertions, and
the grouped construction attains it. That is both bounds for H.

For the campaign: 349,872 fixed-order loading checks against an independent dynamic program,
988 instances proved optimal by two independent methods with no disagreement, 41,514 pairwise
method comparisons with no disagreement, and 16,920 canonical outcomes audited.

On the configuration models: PCF prime's relaxation is proved equal to q, and none of the
2,108 recorded roots exceeds it. PTF can be stronger and exceeds q on two of 232 roots, rising
from five to six where the true optima are twelve and thirteen -- real, but too rare and too
small to close the certificate gap.

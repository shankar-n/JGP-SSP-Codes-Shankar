First, the honest answer

Is any of it substantial?

You asked whether the work is correct, substantial, or even scientific. Those are three different questions and they have three different answers.

Correct

Yes, and it is checkable. Every quantitative claim is reproduced by a program that shares no code with the solvers. Across 41,673 pairs of methods that both proved an optimum on the same instance, none disagreed. The loading rule is verified against an exact dynamic program on 83,670 instance-and-sequence pairs. Two errors were found this way and both are fixed and documented.

Scientific

Yes, more than most. Every statement carries a label used strictly — a Proposition has a proof, an Observation was computed, a Conjecture has neither. Negative results are reported as negative rather than buried. The failed runs are counted in the tables. That discipline is the thing a jury will notice first.

Substantial

The theory is. The methods are not competitive, and that is the finding. Section 4 alone holds two theorems, twelve propositions, four corollaries, four lemmas, five observations, three conjectures and one open problem. Every question your advisors set has an answer. What you do not have is a solver that beats the literature — the multicommodity model closes 1,038 instances against 727 for your best Benders configuration. Do not claim otherwise. The reason it loses is the result.

Publishable

One piece plausibly is — the frame distinction together with the gap analysis. That is Wagler's call, not yours and not mine. Bring it to her as a question, not a claim.

What the 25 minutes should be about

One quantity runs the whole problem

You said the presentation has to be one thing. It is this, and it holds the theory and the computation together:

The elementary counting bound q = |U| − b decides both how good the standard industrial heuristic is, and whether any exact method can close an instance.

Tools that some job needs, minus what fits in the magazine. One line of arithmetic. Everything else in your internship orbits it.

Act 1 — the hook

The textbook example is wrong

The literature's canonical bad case for the two-phase heuristic is the ring family. Under the method as actually specified, every ring from k=3 to 9 has gap zero. The published gaps are walk gaps — one fixed magazine per group. The method's own final loading step closes them.

Act 2 — the theory

The gap is a price, and you can name it

The optimum equals the cheapest walk over all groupings. So the two-phase gap is exactly what you pay for insisting on the fewest groups — nothing else. Zero-gap classes for every capacity, worst-case bounds, the extremal case at three groups.

Act 3 — the close

The same number stops the exact side

Three unrelated formulations — your Benders master, your PCF′ column generation, and the literature's strongest compact model — all reach exactly q. Where q is tight the solver closes 94% of instances; where it is loose, 40%.

The picture the whole talk hangs on

If you can draw this at the board without notes, you can defend the contribution. Six jobs in a ring, magazine holds three tools.

The alternative, if the jury is more applied than theoretical: make Act 3 the subject and Acts 1–2 the setup. Same thesis, more measurement. Ask your advisors which room you are walking into — they know the jury and you do not.

Sorting the pile

What worked, and what didn't

The report keeps everything. The presentation keeps only the left column — and one item from the right, because a measured negative is worth more than a hedge.

Worked — presentable

  • The frame distinction. H and H_walk are different quantities and the literature conflates them. Every ring has heuristic gap zero.
  • Grouping exactness. The optimum is the cheapest walk over all groupings, so the gap is exactly the price of the fewest-groups restriction.
  • Zero-gap classes and worst-case bounds for every capacity, and the exact extremal behaviour at three groups.
  • The census. Positive gaps are rare, always equal to one, and always need b ≥ 4 — which independently matches the one industrial study in the literature.
  • The setup-cost spectrum theorem. The SSP and the grouping problem are two ends of one parameterised model. This answers a question Burger et al. posed and left open.
  • The clutter analysis — negative, but clean and well-posed.
  • The bound-limited diagnosis, established four independent ways.
  • The validation itself. 41,673 cross-method comparisons with zero disagreements is unusually strong for a student project.

Didn't work — report only

  • Fractional Benders cuts. 67.5 million generated, node count down twentyfold, bound never improved, 67 solved instances lost. Keep this one — it is the single most convincing slide you have for the bound-limited thesis.
  • Pareto-optimal cut lifting. Costs 37 instances.
  • The conflict-graph row. Neutral, as predicted.
  • The Benders solver as a competitor. 727 against 1,038. It is a diagnostic instrument, not a faster solver — present it that way.
  • Branch-and-price at scale. Nothing above nine jobs. That is the Python pricer, not the formulation, and you must say which.
  • PTF's advantage in practice. Three runs out of 233, one unit each.
  • The learned cut-selection prototype. Never run on the SSP at all — it was trained on knapsack cover cuts. Leave it out of the talk entirely, or one backup slide.

Twenty minutes is a long time

What they will ask, and what is true

None of these answers requires you to overstate anything. Every one of them is defensible from the report as it stands.

Your solver doesn't beat the state of the art. So what is the contribution?

The contribution is the reason it doesn't, established four independent ways. Three unrelated formulations reach exactly the same elementary bound. The search is not the bottleneck — where the bound is loose, the solver already holds the optimum two-thirds of the time and cannot certify it. Every strengthening aimed at the bound made things worse; only the one aimed at the incumbent helped. That result rules out a family of approaches, which is worth knowing before someone spends a year on it.

How do you know these numbers are right?

An independent verification program that shares no code with the solvers reproduces every quantitative claim. Across 41,673 pairs of methods that both proved an optimum, none disagreed. The loading rule is checked against an exact dynamic program on 83,670 instance-and-sequence pairs. Two analysis errors were caught this way and both are documented in the report rather than quietly fixed.

Isn't the ring counterexample already known?

The example is known; the gap reported for it is a walk gap. Under the two-phase method as actually specified — group, sequence, then re-optimise the loading across the whole flattened order — every ring from k=3 to 9 has gap zero. The standard method is better than the standard model of it. That distinction is what makes the rest of the analysis correct.

Why did the PORTA enumeration not finish?

It was sampled rather than enumerated exhaustively. Say exactly that. It is the one question your advisors set that does not have a complete answer, and pretending otherwise is far more damaging than the gap itself.

Why is the branch-and-price so slow?

Because it is Python with a Python pricer, benchmarked against models compiled into CPLEX. Its solve counts are a lower bound on what the formulations can do and the report says so. The quantity it reports that is free of that problem is the root relaxation, which is why every conclusion drawn from it rests on the bound and not on solve counts.

What would you do next?

Window inequalities — charging |∪T_j| − b over sets of consecutive jobs, which generalises the pairwise and triplet rows in one family. That is the only direction the measurements point to. And the open problem: is the excess of the PTF relaxation over q bounded by a constant? If it is not, that construction becomes worth pursuing.

How much of this did you do yourself?

The report already answers this in §9 and §1.3 — the research directions were set by your advisors, each attributed by name, and the use of AI tools is declared. You do not need a better answer than the honest one. What you need is to be able to defend every claim on every slide, because that is what actually distinguishes understanding from transcription — and that is precisely what the nine days above are for. Do the drills and this question stops being frightening.
